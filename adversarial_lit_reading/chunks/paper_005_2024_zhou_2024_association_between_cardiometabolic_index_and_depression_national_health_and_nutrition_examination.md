A B S T R A C T  
Background: Emerging evidence suggests a common pathophysiological basis for metabolic disorders and mental 
diseases. Despite the existence of reports suggesting a strong connection between dyslipidemia and depression, a 
comprehensive and reliable indicator to identify depression is still lacking. Cardiometabolic index (CMI) is an 
integrated index calculated from three vital metabolic indicators, including triglyceride (TG), high-density li­
poprotein cholesterol (HDL–C) and waist height ratio (WHtR). 
Objective: This study aims to explore the association between CMI and depression. 
Methods: Cross-sectional data of participants with complete information of CMI, depression, and other covariates 
were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Weighted student’s t-test 
and Chi-square test were used to identify the differences between two groups. Weighted multivariate logistic 
regression model, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis, subgroup analysis and interaction tests were 
conducted to explore the association between CMI and depression. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis and 
area under the curve (AUC) were also utilized to evaluate the performance of CMI in identifying depression. 
Results: A positive correlation between CMI and depression was observed in 3794 participants included in the 
study, which was further confirmed to be non-linear via RCS regression analysis, with two significant inflection 
points being identified, including 0.9522 and 1.58. In the crude or adjusted models, individuals with a CMI level 
≥0.9522 exhibited remarkably increased risk for developing depression. CMI got an AUC of 0.748 in identifying 
depression. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests indicate that the association between CMI and depression 
remained consistent across different subgroups and was not modified by other covariates except drinking. Those 
who are current drinkers and with a high CMI are more susceptible to suffer depression. 
Conclusions: An elevated CMI is linked to increased risk for depression. Addressing dyslipidemia and improving 
lipid levels may potentially lower the risk for depression.

1. Introduction 
Depression is deemed as one of the most incapacitating psychological 
disorders and is correlated with significant individual, societal, and 
economic burdens (Bromet et al., 2011; Li et al., 2023). In light of the 
COVID-19 pandemic, there has been a significant surge in the global 
incidence of depression (Salari et al., 2020). It is undoubtedly that the 
depressive disorders have become a major health burden across different 
regions and ethnic groups. Currently, the management of depression 
encompasses various modalities such as psychotherapy, pharmaco­
therapy, electroconvulsive therapy and integrative therapy. Notwith­
standing the broad and comparatively efficacious application of 
antidepressants in clinical settings, they frequently yield adverse effects 
and necessitate weeks before exhibiting effectiveness, thereby limiting 
their usefulness in specific populations, including those with acute 
conditions, adolescents, and pregnant women (Han et al., 2023; 
Jakobsen et al., 2020). Consequently, the prediction and prevention of 
depression is of paramount importance. Moreover, in current clinical 
practice, there is an urgent requirement for a reliable, accurate and 
easily accessible indicator to evaluate the onset and progression of 
depressive disorders. 
The pathogenesis of depression is complex and can be modified by 
various factors, ranging from genetic, psychological, environmental to 
biological factors. In recent years, the relationship between metabolic 
profile with mental health has received increasing attention from re­
searchers, and amounting evidences have revealed a common patho­
physiological basis underlying the two conditions (Al-Khatib et al., 
2022). The data from a meta-analysis suggested that individuals com­
bined with an adverse metabolic profile (e.g., lipometabolic distur­
bance, glycometabolic disorder) obtained significantly higher risks for 
the development of depressive symptoms (Jokela et al., 2014). Previous 
research has indicated that individuals with depressive symptoms often 
exhibit specific changes in their lipid profiles, such as decreased levels of 
HDL, increased levels of LDL and triglycerides, and elevated levels of 
total cholesterol (TC) (Khalfan et al., 2023). Additionally, studies 
focusing on adults with major depressive disorder (MDD) have found 
that the severity of dyslipidemia and hypertriglyceridemia is signifi­
cantly associated with the severity of depressive symptoms, including 
suicidal ideation (Oh and Kim, 2017; So et al., 2021). For instance, a 
study involving 601 adults with MDD found that a high ratio of tri­
glycerides to HDL was positively correlated with an increased risk of 
developing MDD over a nine-year follow-up period (Watson et al., 
2021). These findings strongly suggest a close association between lip­
ometabolic disturbances and depressive disorders. Therefore, indicators 
that can effectively reflect changes in an individual’s lipid profile may 
serve as useful biomarkers for depressive disorders. 
The diagnosis of lipometabolic disturbances involves the integration 
of anthropometric and biochemical parameters. Therefore, some in­
dicators for lipid metabolism profile have been established, such as 
visceral adiposity index (VAI) (Lazzer et al., 2023), which incorporates 
waist circumference (WC) and body mass index (BMI), and TG/HDL-C 
ratio (Kosmas et al., 2023), which integrates triglyceride (TG)and 
high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and the cardiometabolic index (CMI) 
(Lazzer et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2021), which effectively integrates the 
marker of adiposity (i.e., waist-to-height ratio/WtHR) with the TG/HDL- 
C ratio. Compared with other indicators described previously, the in­
dexes of VAI and TG/HDL-C ratio have been shown to reflect lipid 
metabolism accurately in children and adolescents with severe obesity, 
as well as among obese adults (Lazzer et al., 2023). Moreover, 
amounting evidences have suggested that these lipometabolic indicators 
are closely linked to depressive disorders with favorable performance 
(Shi et al., 2021, pp. 2005–2018; So et al., 2021). However, as an in­
tegrated and comprehensive index based on anthropometric and 
biochemical parameters, whether CMI is closely connected with 
depression still remains unclear. Since the metabolic status of in­
dividuals with lipometabolic disturbance worsens over the years, 
effective monitoring of lipometabolic disorders in depressive individuals 
is critical for developing an effective intervention to prevent the adverse 
health outcomes. 
Hence, the objective of this study was to investigate the link between 
CMI and depression using the cross-sectional data from the NHANES 
database. The findings from our study could offer persuasive evidence 
regarding the role of lipid metabolism in depression and serve as a 
valuable reference for individuals and healthcare professionals in their 
daily practice. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Data accession and study population 
This research was conducted with data sourced from the National 
Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) carried out from 
2011 to 2014. The NHANES is conducted by the National Center for 
Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Pre­
vention (CDC) in the United States. The NHANES survey collects data via 
trained experts from interviews and examinations to create a represen­
tative sample of the US population using a complex multistage, strati­
fied, clustered probability sample. The survey protocol has been duly 
sanctioned by the Institutional Review Board of the CDC, and all par­
ticipants have voluntarily given their informed consent. 
Two cycles of dataset (2011–2012, 2013–2014) with full information 
were used for analysis. There was a total of 19,932 participants in the 
NHANES during 2011–2014. We have excluded individuals who failed 
to complete the survey or had missing or unreliable CMI data (n =
14,054) and PHQ-9 data (n = 1265). Besides, participants without 
complete information about BMI, education, marital status, PIR, smok­
ing and drinking were also excluded. As a result, a total of 3794 par­
ticipants have been included in our final analysis. The flowchart for 
participant screening was presented in Fig. 1.

consists of nine items that capture the signs and symptoms of depression

4-point scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day) (Cai

2.3. Covariates 
The covariates were selected based on previous literature and sub­
stantive reasoning that suggested their potential association with both 
CMI levels and depression prevalence. Standardized questionnaires 
were utilized to gather information on various factors, including age, 
gender, race, education level, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), 
body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol use, and cardiovascular 
disease (CVD) history. Race was categorized into four groups: Mexican 
American, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic White, and other races. 
PIR was stratified into three levels: <1.30, 1.30–3.49, and ≥3.50. 
Marital status was categorized into three groups: married, unmarried, 
and others (widowed, divorced, and separated). Education was stratified 
into five levels: less than 9th grade, 9th–11th grade, high school, some 
college, and college or above. Participants were classified as past 
smokers or current smokers based on their responses to questions about 
smoking at least 100 cigarettes during their lifetime and whether they 
were currently smoking. Alcohol status was determined based on a 
question that “Had at least 12 alcohol drinks a year?”. CVD was assessed 
by a set of self-reported questionnaires. The Chronic Kidney Disease 
Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation was used to diagnose 
chronic kidney disease (CKD) based on an estimated glomerular filtra­
tion rate (eGFR) of <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 or albumin-to-creatinine 
Ratio (ACR) ≥30 mg/g (Wang et al., 2016). Hypertension was defined 
by participants reporting a diagnosis, having an average systolic blood 
pressure (SBP) of ≥130 mmHg or an average diastolic blood pressure 
(DBP) of ≥80 mmHg, or taking antihypertensive medication (Peng et al., 
2023, pp. 2011–2018). Diabetes can be diagnosed through physician 
confirmation or by meeting specific criteria. These criteria include 
having a glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level >6.5 %, a fasting 
glucose level of 7.00 mmol/L or higher, a random blood glucose level of 
11.10 mmol/L or higher, a two-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 
result of 11.10 mmol/L or higher, or current use of diabetes medication 
or insulin (Ou et al., 2023, p. 2). Detailed information on the specimen 
collection, processing, quality assurance, and monitoring are described 
in the section of biospecimen program in NHANES.

value <0.05.

3. Results 
3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population 
The baseline characteristics of all participants based on grouping of 
depression were presented in Table 1. The mean age of all enrolled 
subjects was 48.38 ± 17.53 years, with 50.47 % of them being female, 
and 44.23 % of them being non-Hispanic white. The weighted preva­
lence of depression was 8.51 % in the entire US population. 
Compared with the non-depressive group, individuals in the 
depressive group exhibited a higher level of CMI (1.30 ± 1.10 vs. 1.04 
± 0.87), LAP (107.52 ± 87.32 vs. 56.43 ± 82.71), VAI (151.83 ±
170.08 vs. 100.69 ± 185.98), TyG-index (9.02 ± 0.75 vs. 8.50 ± 0.64), 
as well as higher levels of age and BMI. Besides, non-depressive partic­
ipants were more likely to have low education levels, low family annual 
income, and be a current drinker, and have multiple chronic co- 
morbidities, such as CVD, CKD, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipid­
emia, and so on. However, there was no significant statistical difference 
in race between people with and without depression. 
3.2. Association between cardiometabolic index (CMI) and depression 
The weighted logistic regression analysis was utilized to explore the 
association between CMI and depression, and the results were presented 
in Table 2. A positive correlation between CMI (as continuous variable 
or categorical variable) and the occurrence of depression was found in 
all models (including the non-adjusted and adjusted models). When CMI 
was analyzed as continuous variable, each unit increase in CMI was 
correlated with a 34 %, 28 % and 34 % increase in odds ratio for the 
occurrence of depression from model 1 to model 3, respectively. After 
converting CMI into a categorical variable, the prevalence of depression 
also increased with increasing tertiles of CMI (P for trend <0.001, in 
three models). In model 3, participants in the highest CMI tertile group 
were more liable to develop depression than those in the middle and low 
CMI tertile group (OR, 2.28 vs. 1.54 vs. 1, P for trend <0.001). 
The associations between other cardiometabolic indicators and 
depression were also explored, including VAI, LAP and TyG-index 
(sTable 1). Similar to CMI, when LAP and TrG-index were analyzed as 
a continuous variable, each unit increase in CMI was significantly 
associated with higher likelihood of depression for LAP and TrG-index in 
all models. However, when LAP and TrG-index were converted to cat­
egorical variables (tertiles), the associations between LAP and TrG-index 
and depression became insignificant in the fully adjusted model, sug­
gesting the instable associations between LAP and TrG-index and 
depression. Regarding VAI, although VAI was significantly associated 
with depression in model 1 and model 2 when VAI was analyzed as 
continuous variable or categorical variable, such association also 
became insignificant in the fully adjusted model (model 3). These results 
together revealed that VAI, LAP and TyG-index cannot get favorable 
performance in discriminating or predicting depression. 
Additionally, the ability of CMI and other three cardiometabolic 
indicators to identify depression was further analyzed by using ROC 
curve (sFig. 1). Compared with other three indicators (with the AUC of 
0.567, 0.552 and 0.516 for TrG-index, VAI and LAP, respectively), CMI 
got a significantly higher AUC of 0.748, suggesting an superior perfor­
mance in identifying depression. 
3.3. Analysis of restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression 
In restricted cubic spline regression, after the adjustment of potential 
covariates, a significant nonlinear relationship between CMI and 
depression (P for nonlinearity <0.001) was detected (Fig. 2). A trend of 
increased OR for depression accompanying by increasing CMI was 
observed between them. RCS analysis identified two significant inflec­
tion points: the first at 0.9522 and the second at 1.58. Individuals with a 
CMI level below 0.9522 had a very low risk of developing depression.

(sFig. 3). In contrast, such phenomena was not observed between 
different genders (sFig. 4). 
4. Discussion 
To our knowledge, this study was the first to examine the association 
between CMI and depression. The results showed that higher CMI levels 
were associated with an increased risk of developing depression, 
regardless of whether CMI was analyzed as a continuous or categorical 
variable. The relationship between CMI and depression was found to be 
non-linear, with two significant inflection points identified at 0.9522 
and 1.58, respectively. Logistic regression analysis confirmed a positive 
correlation between higher CMI and depression when CMI was analyzed 
as a three categorical variable (divided by the inflection points of 0.9522 
and 1.58). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests revealed that the 
association between CMI and depression was consistent across different 
subgroups and not influenced by other covariates except for drinking. 
The positive correlation between CMI and depression was more pro­
nounced in those with who current drinkers. 
The metabolic profile that leads to depression has attracted the 
attention of scientists in recent years (Catoira et al., 2016). As an indi­
cator for evaluating metabolic profile by assessing both abdominal 
obesity and blood lipid (Miao et al., 2023), our results observed a robust 
association between higher levels of CMI and increased occurrence of 
depression. There are several reasons that may account for such strong 
association. Firstly, the inflammatory response caused by abdominal 
obesity and increased blood lipids may leads to such association (Guo 
et al., 2023). Amounting evidences have suggested that abdominal 
obesity and increased blood lipids are often accompanied by an increase 
in chronic inflammation, which may affect the nervous system and 
emotional regulation, and thus leads to depressive disorders (Liu et al., 
2022; Shelton and Miller, 2010). Secondly, metabolic disorders usually 
result in the imbalance of the neuroendocrine system (Kivim¨aki et al., 
2023), such as insulin resistance, increased secretion of glucocorticoid, 
and disturbance of glucocorticoid sensitivity associated with systemic 
cortisol activity (Jakobsen et al., 2020; Steptoe and Frank, 2023). These 
changes may interfere with neurogenesis and neural plasticity, and thus 
cause depression. Thirdly, the hormonal alterations were not only 
associated with metabolic changes, but also can impact the neural inputs 
of the reward circuit, which may ultimately result in the manifestation 
of anhedonia and thus depression (Bangasser and Cuarenta, 2021). Be­
sides, since high CMI levels were reported to usually coexist with various 
additional risk factors, such as oxidative stress, self-esteem, social sup­
port, and the capacity to modify self-efficacy, and so on (Koborov´a et al., 
2018; Monserrat-Mesquida et al., 2023; ´Swiątkiewicz et al., 2023). 
Therefore, these risk factors may collectively contribute to an elevated 
susceptibility to developing depression (Kandola et al., 2019). Given 
that the strong association between CMI and depression, in clinical 
contexts, it is crucial to conduct a thorough assessment of individuals 
with elevated CMI levels and to adopt efficacious interventions for 
mitigating risk factors, thereby reducing the likelihood of depression. 
The present study revealed a significant association between higher 
CMI levels and an increased prevalence of depression, even after 
adjusting for potential confounding variables. This suggests that a more 
severe disturbance in metabolic profile is correlated with a higher 
incidence of depression. Interestingly, this association between CMI 
levels and depression prevalence remained consistent across most sub­
groups. Notably, individuals categorized as obese or overweight (with a 
BMI ≥25 kg/m2) showed a robust association between CMI levels and 
depression prevalence, unlike their non-obese or non-overweight 
counterparts. Surprisingly, individuals with hyperlipidemia had a 
lower depression prevalence and lower CMI levels compared to the 
population without hyperlipidemia. However, within the hyperlipid­
emia group, a higher level of CMI was associated with a higher incidence 
of depression. These findings highlight the importance of considering 
multiple factors, including unhealthy lifestyle habits and biological 
changes, when addressing abdominal obesity, hyperlipidemia, and 
depression.

cant when the drinking status was divided into three subgroups, 
including never drinker, former drinker and current drinker. When the

ciation between CMI and depression was significantly modified by 
drinking status. Actually, drinking has already been reported to be 
closely associated with CMI and depression in numerous previous

found that the relationship between CMI and depression was more

former drinkers (sFig. 3). The RCS curve reveals that the OR for 
depression increases significantly as CMI continues to elevate in the

former drinkers group, the OR for depression decreases smoothly as CMI 
exceeds the value of approximate 1.5, a point at which subjects get the 
highest OR for depression. In our study, since the drinking status was got 
from the Alcohol Use Questionnaire (variable name prefix ALQ), which 
was focusing on the lifetime and current use (past 12 months) of alcohol, 
and the questions are not specific to type of alcohol used, it is very 
difficult to define the detail status of drinking as reported in previous 
studies. Secondly, previous studies have reported that increasing 
involvement with alcohol significantly increases the risk of depression 
(Boden and Fergusson, 2011; Zhang et al., 2021), and individuals with 
depressive symptoms or anxiety symptoms are more likely to have un­
healthy patterns of alcohol drinking (Johannessen et al., 2017), sug­
gesting that drinking and depression can affect each other. Therefore, 
alcohol drinking is a vital variable which may influence the relationship 
between CMI and depression, which should be taken into account when 
CMI is used as an index related to the risk of depression. Further studies 
are still needed to elucidate the detailed relationship between drinking, 
CMI and depression in the future. 
The current study has several key strengths that contribute to the 
reliability and significance of the findings. Firstly, this study is the first 
to investigate the association between CMI levels and depression prev­
alence, providing novel insights into the relationship between metabolic 
profile and mental health. Secondly, the use of NHANES data, which 
employs a rigorous multistage probability sampling design, ensures that 
the results are representative of the broader U.S. population. This en­
hances the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Thirdly, the 
study has taken into account potential confounding factors, allowing for 
a more accurate assessment of the association between CMI levels and 
depression prevalence. Consequently, the results have important im­
plications for public health strategies aimed at preventing depression. 
There are several limitations in our study that should be acknowl­
edged. Firstly, the study sample was limited to specific demographic 
areas, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other 
populations. Conducting large-scale studies that include a more diverse 
range of regions and populations would enhance the external validity of 
the results. Secondly, the sample size in our study was relatively small, 
which may affect the reliability and generalizability of the findings. 
Replicating the study with a larger sample size would provide more 
robust evidence. Additionally, there is a need to standardize and 
compare the various methods used to assess metabolic profile to ensure 
consistency and comparability of results across studies. Thirdly, the 
cross-sectional design of our study limits our ability to establish cau­
sality between CMI levels and depression prevalence. Conducting long- 
term randomized controlled trials would provide more definitive evi­
dence for establishing a causal relationship between CMI levels and 
depression prevalence. Collectively, while our study provides valuable 
insights, these limitations should be taken into account when inter­
preting the results. 
5. Conclusion 
In conclusion, our investigation utilizing a nationally representative 
cohort of US population revealed a robust positive correlation between 
CMI and depression. A positive, and non-linear association between CMI 
and depression was observed by RCS analysis, with two significant in­
flection points being identified, including 0.9522 and 1.58. Individuals 
with a higher level of CMI is significantly associated with increased risk 
for developing depression. These findings suggest that CMI can serve as 
a useful indicator for assessing the risk of depression in individuals with 
dyslipidemia. Besides, maintaining a CMI level below 0.9522 may help 
to reduce the risk of developing depression.
