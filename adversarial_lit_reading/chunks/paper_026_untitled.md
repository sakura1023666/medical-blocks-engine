BACKGROUND: Cardiac, renal, and metabolic (CRM) conditions are major causes of morbidity and mortality globally. This study 
aims to explore the relationship between serum uric acid (SUA), hyperuricemia, gout, and CRM conditions in middle-­aged and 
elderly populations.
METHODS: Sample 1 included participants from CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, n=9341), and 
Sample 2 from NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, unweighted n=17 913; weighted n=115 646 390). 
Ordinal logistic regression, Cox regression, and restricted cubic spline analyses were used to assess the relationship between 
SUA, hyperuricemia, gout, and CRM conditions. A 2-­sample Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted to explore 
causal associations between SUA and CRM conditions.
RESULTS: In both samples, SUA, hyperuricemia, and gout were positively correlated with the risk of CRM conditions. Among 
participants with 3 or ≥1 CRM condition(s), SUA, asymptomatic hyperuricemia, and gout with poorly controlled hyperuricemia 
showed significant positive associations with all-­cause mortality, whereas these associations were not observed in patients 
with gout with normal SUA levels. The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a positive relationship between SUA levels and 
the risk of all-­cause mortality in participants with ≥1 CRM condition(s), demonstrating a nonlinear dose–response relationship 
across both samples (P for nonlinearity <0.05). Mendelian randomization analysis indicated that SUA was causally associated 
with cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes.
CONCLUSIONS: Hyperuricemia and gout are strong predictors of increased prevalence and mortality of CRM conditions, 
emphasizing the importance of managing hyperuricemia and gout in these patients.

accounting for approximately one third of all deaths 
worldwide.3 The interconnected nature of these con-
ditions suggests that dysfunction in one system can 
trigger or worsen dysfunction in others. Among adults 
in the United States, the estimated prevalence of indi-

cardiovascular disease (CVD), 15% for chronic kidney 
disease (CKD), and 13% for diabetes, with >1 in 4 in-

Given the significant cumulative associations between 
coexisting CRM conditions and adverse outcomes, 
including mortality, disability, and economic burden,4 
optimizing care pathways to mitigate their impact is 
crucial. Regular exercise, along with well-­controlled 
blood pressure, serum glucose, and lipid levels, can 
significantly improve the prognosis of patients with 
CRM conditions and their widely recognized risk fac-
tors, including dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, hyperten-
sion, and obesity.5,6
Uric acid is the final product of purine metabo-
lism, primarily synthesized in the liver and excreted 
through the renal tract and intestines.7 At physiological 
concentrations, circulating uric acid acts as an anti-
oxidant and can scavenge reactive oxygen species.8 
Hyperuricemia, traditionally associated with gout, is 
now recognized as a key contributor to cardiovascular 
and metabolic diseases, including hypertension, CVD, 
CKD, diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia, and metabolic 
syndrome.9 Elevated serum uric acid (SUA) is often 
linked with insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and dys-
lipidemia, suggesting its involvement in the metabolic 
environment underlying CRM conditions.10 The patho-
genic effects of SUA are largely mediated through its 
proinflammatory and prooxidative properties. Elevated 
SUA induces oxidative stress by activating NADPH ox-
idase, leading to increased production of reactive oxy-
gen species.11 It also triggers inflammatory responses 
by upregulating mediators such as monocyte chemo-
attractant protein-­1, C-­reactive protein, and promoting 
platelet activation.12 These processes result in endo-
thelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and insulin 
resistance, which together contribute to the develop-
ment and progression of CVD, CKD, and diabetes. 
Additionally, SUA may indirectly influence CRM condi-
tions through its association with metabolic risk factors 
such as obesity and dyslipidemia, further exacerbating 
disease pathways. These mechanisms highlight SUA’s 
dual role as both a direct pathogen and an indirect 
amplifier of metabolic dysfunction. Due to changes in 
dietary patterns, improved living conditions, and aging 
populations, the global prevalence and disease burden 
of hyperuricemia and gout have been steadily increas-
ing.13 However, despite the growing understanding of 
SUA’s contribution to CRM pathogenesis, its inter-
action with gout, other metabolic factors, and CRM 
outcomes—particularly in middle-­aged and older pop-
ulations—remains underexplored.
This study aims to investigate these relationships 
using data from 2 large databases, CHARLS (China 
Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study) and 
NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination 
Survey), and to assess the causal association between 
SUA and CRM conditions through Mendelian random-
ization (MR) analysis.

•	 This study establishes a clear link between

with the risk of cardiac, renal, and metabolic

Sample 1 included participants from the CHARLS 
database. CHARLS is a national population-­based 
survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years or older, con-
ducted by the National School of Development at 
Peking University. Participants were selected using a 
4-­stage, stratified, cluster random sampling method 
from 150 counties across 28 provinces.14 This study 
used cross-­sectional data from wave 3 of the CHARLS 
survey, including 9341 eligible participants. Figure S1 
shows the participant selection process.
Sample 2 included participants from the NHANES 
database. NHANES, conducted by the Centers for 
Disease Control and Prevention, collects data on the 
health and nutritional status of the US population every 
2 years. It selects representative samples of nonin-
stitutionalized civilians.15 Participants in this analysis 
were from 6 2-­year survey cycles conducted between 
2007 and 2018. The participant screening process is 
shown in Figure S2. A large, nationally representative 
sample of US adults aged ≥45 years was identified (un-
weighted n=17 913; weighted n=115 646 390), including 
1314 (7.3%) patients with gout.
Study Variables
The variables for both samples included age (years), 
sex (male, female), body mass index (BMI) catego-
ries (Sample 1: <24 kg/m2, 24–28 kg/m2, ≥28 kg/m2; 
Sample 2: <25 kg/m2, 25–30 kg/m2, ≥30 kg/m2), educa-
tion level (Sample 1: less than lower secondary, upper 
secondary and vocational training, tertiary; Sample 
2: less than 9th grade, 9th–11th grade, high school 
graduate, college or associate degree, college gradu-
ate or above), marital status (Sample 1: married/living 
with partner, widowed/divorced/separated; Sample 2: 
married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/sepa-
rated, never married), estimated glomerular filtration 
rate (calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease 
Epidemiology Collaboration equation),16 low-­density li-
poprotein cholesterol (mmol/L), triglycerides (mmol/L), 
SUA (mg/dL), and smoking history (yes, no). The history 
of hypertension (yes, no), CVD (yes, no), CKD (yes, no), 
and diabetes (yes, no) were also included. Additionally, 
Sample 1 included residence (rural or urban) as a vari-
able. Sample 2 included additional variables such as 
race or ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, 
non-­Hispanic White, non-­Hispanic Black, other race 
or ethnicity, such as Non-Hispanic Asian and Multi-
Racial), poverty:income ratio, and history of gout (yes, 
no). Poverty:income ratio was used to define income, 
with poverty defined as a poverty:income ratio <0.99.
In CHARLS, blood samples were collected from 
participants in the Venous Blood-­Based Biomarkers 
in the China Health study after an overnight fast by 
uniformly trained staff from the Chinese Center for 
Disease Control and Prevention, following standard 
protocols. SUA levels were measured using the UA 
Plus method.17 In NHANES, SUA levels were mea-
sured using the DxC800 synchron with a timed end 
point method.18
Regarding survival status, the latest release of 
CHARLS Wave 5 (2020) data provided survival sta-
tus (alive or deceased) for participants from Wave 3. 
However, specific dates of death were not provided for 
deceased participants. For these individuals, survival 
time was estimated by calculating the median time 
from T2 to T3, combined with the time from baseline 
to T2.
The National Center for Health Statistics linked data 
from various surveys with death certificate records 
from the National Death Index, with follow-­up through 
NHANES 1999 to 2018. The Linked Mortality Files 
were updated with mortality data through December 
31, 2019. In NHANES, follow-­up time is defined as the 
time from the interview to the date of death or the end 
of the follow-­up period.19
Definition
Hyperuricemia is defined as an SUA level greater 
than 7 mg/dL in men and postmenopausal women, 
or >6 mg/dL in premenopausal women.20 In this 
study, hyperuricemia was defined as an SUA level >7 
mg/dL for all middle-­aged and older participants.21 
Asymptomatic hyperuricemia was defined as an SUA 
level >7 mg/dL, with individuals answering “No” to the 
question, “Has a doctor ever told you that you had 
gout?” Gout was defined as answering “Yes” to the 
question, “Has a doctor ever told you that you had 
gout?” CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular 
filtration rate <60 or the presence of albuminuria (urine 
albumin-­creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g).22 Diabetes was 
defined as either a self-­reported diagnosis by a health 
care professional or a glycated hemoglobin level of at 
least 6.5%. CVD was defined as self-­reported diag-
nosis of 1 or more of the following by a health care 
professional: heart failure, coronary heart disease, 
myocardial infarction, or stroke.1

the genetic instrument for exposure must be strongly 
associated with the trait of interest. Second, the se-

through any other pathways independent of the expo-

10 000 kb) were excluded. A total of 268 SNVs strongly 
associated with SUA were selected for causality anal-
ysis, with the proportion of variance in SUA levels 
explained by the SNVs estimated at 8.53%. Detailed 
information regarding data downloading and screening 
is provided in Table S1 and S2. All selected SNVs had 
an F-­statistic >10.
We used the LD Link web tool (https://​ldlink.​nih.​
gov/?​tab=​home) to assess the associations of the 
268 SNVs with confounding factors and outcomes. 
SNVs associated with confounding factors (eg, BMI, 
renal function, educational attainment, hypertension, 
smoking, triglycerides) and outcomes (CVD, CKD, 
and diabetes) were excluded, leaving a total of 176 
SNVs. Of these 176 SNVs, 155 were directly matched 
in the summary data of SNV–outcome genome-­wide 
association study. We then excluded palindromic 
SNVs, leaving 148 SNVs for SUA on CVD, 149 for SUA 
on CKD, and 149 for SUA on diabetes as instrumental 
variables.
Statistical Analysis
Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD. 
Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to 
assess differences in continuous variables between 
groups, and the chi-­square test was used for categori-
cal variables. To account for the complex sampling de-
sign and ensure nationally representative estimates, all 
analyses for Sample 2 were adjusted for survey design 
and weighting variables.25 Multinomial ordinal logistic 
regression models were used to estimate the asso-
ciation between SUA, hyperuricemia, and the number 
of CRM conditions. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs 
were calculated using multivariable Cox proportional 
hazards regression models to assess the associa-
tions of SUA levels and hyperuricemia with all-­cause 
mortality risk, adjusting for potential confounders. Age, 
sex, and race were adjusted for in model 1, and covari-
ates with significant differences between the 2 groups 
were further adjusted for in model 2. Due to sample 
size limitations and the number of outcome events, Cox 
regression analysis was limited to participants with 0 
CRM or ≥1 CRM in Sample 1. In Sample 2, we also 
analyzed the relationship between gout and CRM con-
ditions, illustrating overall survival time (in months) using 
Kaplan–Meier curves, which were compared with 
a log-­rank test based on the number of CRM condi-
tions. Additionally, restricted cubic spline analysis was 
performed to examine the dose–response relationship 
between SUA levels and all-­cause mortality. This rela-
tionship was further assessed through stratified analy-
ses by sex (male, female), age groups (45–64, 65 years 
and older), and BMI categories (Sample 1: <24 kg/m2, 
24–28 kg/m2, ≥28 kg/m2; Sample 2: <25 kg/m2, 25–30 
kg/m2, ≥30 kg/m2).
For the 2-­sample MR analysis, the inverse variance 
weighted method was the primary approach used to 
assess causal associations between SUA and CRM 
conditions. The MR Egger method and weighted me-
dian method were also employed. Cochran’s Q test 
was used to estimate heterogeneity between SNVs. 
The intercept of MR-­Egger was used to assess plei-
otropy of the genetic variants. We used MR Pleiotropy 
Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-­PRESSO) to evaluate 
the extent of horizontal pleiotropy. Outlier variants 
identified by the MR-­PRESSO outlier test were re-
moved step by step to reduce heterogeneity and the 
effect of horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-­one-­out analy-
sis was performed to determine whether the causal 
estimate was influenced by a single SNV. This study 
adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. All statistical 
analyses were performed using R 4.4.1 and SPSS 23. 
A 2-­sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically 
significant.

This study, for the first time, integrated data from 
the CHARLS and NHANES databases to system-
atically investigate the associations between SUA, 
hyperuricemia, gout, and CRM conditions. Using 
various statistical approaches and MR analyses, we 
confirmed the robustness of our findings. The study 
revealed significant positive associations between 
SUA, hyperuricemia, gout, and CRM conditions, 
with higher numbers of CRM conditions correlating 
with increased all-­cause mortality. Dose–response 
analyses demonstrated a nonlinear positive relation-

Furthermore, MR confirmed the causal associations 
of SUA with CVD, CKD, and diabetes. This research

Some observational studies have shown that el-
evated SUA levels are an independent risk factor for 
CVD, including hypertension, coronary artery disease, 
atrial fibrillation, and heart failure.26,27 Research sug-
gests that hyperuricemia promotes the onset and pro-
gression of CVD through several mechanisms. First, 
hyperuricemia activates the renin-­angiotensin system, 
leading to vasoconstriction and sodium retention, 
which contribute to the development of hypertension.28 
Second, urate salts participate in vascular inflamma-
tion and oxidative stress by increasing the oxidation 
of low-­density lipoprotein, lipid peroxidation, and C-­
reactive protein levels, thereby damaging endothelial 
cells and promoting the progression of hypertension.29 
Additionally, urate salts activate the mitogen-­activated 
protein kinase pathway, inducing cyclooxygenase-­2 
expression and upregulating platelet-­derived growth 
factor, which stimulates the proliferation and migration 
of vascular smooth muscle cells, ultimately leading to 
vascular remodeling and the progression of CVD.30,31 
In kidney disease, the deposition of urate salts in the 
renal tubules and interstitium significantly inhibits the 
expression of nitric oxide synthase in the kidney. The 
reduction in nitric oxide disrupts the regulation of 
renal vascular tone, renal blood flow, and glomerular 
feedback.32 Furthermore, urate directly affects en-
dothelial cells by decreasing nitric oxide levels and 
triggering vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, 
extracellular matrix deposition, and macrophage ad-
hesion and migration.33 These processes contribute 
to increased arterial resistance and renal dysfunction. 
Moreover, hyperuricemia is closely associated with in-
sulin resistance through mechanisms such as direct 
cytotoxic effects on pancreatic β-­cells, reduced insulin 
secretion, and decreased insulin receptor sensitivity. 
Insulin resistance, in turn, exacerbates hyperuricemia 
by upregulating the expression of URAT1 (urate trans-
porter 1) in the renal cortex.34,35 It is noteworthy that 
gout is a stronger risk factor for mortality than isolated 
hyperuricemia. Previous clinical studies on gout have 
also shown a high prevalence of chronic comorbidities, 
consistent with the findings of this study.36–38 Gout, 
characterized by monosodium urate crystal deposition 
in joints, represents a clinical manifestation of elevated 
SUA levels. In addition to the previously discussed 
role of SUA in promoting CRM, the inflammatory re-
sponse associated with gout constitutes another crit-
ical mechanism contributing to CRM. Monosodium 
urate crystal deposition induces robust inflammatory 
responses through direct neutrophil activation and the 
NACHT-LRR-PYD-containing protein 3 inflammasome 
pathway, resulting in the release of proinflammatory cy-
tokines during acute gout attacks.39 Recent evidence 
suggests that this inflammation persists between at-
tacks, with ultrasound studies confirming ongoing

In our study, discrepancies were observed in the strat-
ified analyses across the 2 data sets. Differences in 
population demographics, ethnicities, or adjusted con-
founding factors may have contributed to the observed 
disparities in the results. To better address these is-
sues, we used MR analysis to establish the causal re-
lationship between SUA and CVD, CKD, and diabetes. 
By effectively eliminating confounding factors, such as 
environmental influences, MR provides more reliable 
causal inferences, helping to overcome the limitations 
of traditional observational studies.
Several limitations should be considered when 
interpreting our findings. The 2 study samples were 
from different countries, which allowed us to explore 
the relationship between uric acid and CRM condi-
tions in diverse ethnic groups. The distinct origins of 
Sample 1 and Sample 2, as well as racial, age, and 
sex variations in the distribution of these samples, 
might have led to some disparities, partly explaining 
the different results observed in the stratified anal-
yses. Despite these variations, the primary findings 
across both samples were consistent, showing a sig-
nificant association between SUA and an increased 
prevalence of CRM conditions and all-­cause mortal-
ity in patients with CRM conditions. This consistency 
across diverse samples strengthens the reliability of 
our conclusions. Data availability prevented us from 
including all potential confounding factors. Due to 
the limited number of outcome events in some sub-
groups, we were able to conduct stratified analyses 
only in participants with 0 or ≥1 CRM condition. The 
participants in our MR analyses were primarily of 
European ancestry. Although minimizing the potential 
bias from population stratification, this may not fully 
represent other ethnic groups.
Conclusions
In conclusion, combining 2 large data sets and MR 
analysis enabled us to find that SUA and gout are both 
strong risk factors for the prevalence and all-­cause 
mortality of CRM conditions. Our results underscore 
the importance of managing hyperuricemia and gout 
in patients with CRM conditions. Causal effects of SUA 
genetic variants on CRM conditions were confirmed 
through MR analyses.
