A B S T R A C T
Environmental pollutant is considered to be one of the important factors affecting adolescent growth. However,
the effects of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) exposure on adolescent growth have not been assessed. Data
from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2018 was used to examine the
associations between VOCs exposure and adolescent growth indicators through three statistical models. The
mediating effect of bone mineral density (BMD) on these associations was examined. The potential pathways and
key targets were identified by the network pharmacology analysis methods. This study included 746 adolescents.
Three statistical methods consistently showed a negative correlation between VOCs exposure and adolescent
growth indicators. Furthermore, BMD mediated the relationship between VOCs exposure and adolescent growth
indicators, with mediated proportion ranging from 4.3 % to 53.4 %. Network pharmacology analysis found a
significant enrichment in IL-17 signaling pathway. Moreover, the adverse effects of VOCs exposure on adolescent
growth were observed to significantly attenuate in adolescents with high serum vitamin D levels. Our results
suggested that VOCs exposure was an adverse factor affecting adolescent growth, with BMD playing a significant

1. Introduction
Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are a type of harmful low mo-
lecular mass carbon-containing compounds that are widespread in the
environment. With the development of industrialization and urbaniza-
tion, the sources of indoor and outdoor VOCs are gradually increasing.
The major sources of personal exposure to VOCs include automotive
exhaust, cooking fumes, cigarettes, insecticides, personal care products,
and other industrial products [1–3]. According to reports, the human
can absorb VOCs through many ways, such as air, food and skin [4,5].
Previous studies have shown that exposure to VOCs is closely related to
the occurrence and development of several diseases, including cardio-
vascular and cerebrovascular diseases, cancer, hypertension, and res-
piratory diseases [6–9]. This emphasizes that the potential threat of
VOCs exposure to human health cannot be ignored. Given that the
biomarkers of VOCs exposure in urine have a longer biological half-life
than that in blood and are more stable during transportation, storage
and handling processes [10,11]. Therefore, the measurements of VOCs
in urine have become reliable indicators for monitoring human VOCs
exposure.
Adolescence is an important period of growth and development. The
growth
and

in

are
related
to
health
throughout the life course [12]. Short stature and stunting have signif-
icant effects on morbidity and mortality in adulthood [13]. Many studies
have shown that adolescent growth and development can be affected by
diet, disease and environmental pollutants [14,15]. Environmental
pollutants can produce multiple adverse effects in both humans and
wildlife by interfering with the endocrine system of organisms [16,17].
Compared with adults, adolescents are more vulnerable to be affected by
the adverse effects of environmental pollutants due to their higher
metabolic and ventilation rates, and underdeveloped immune systems
[18]. In a study of adolescents in the US, Wan et al. [19] found that
exposure to higher PFAS levels affected growth and development in
adolescents. Another cohort study of 212 Canadian adolescents showed
that peri-pubertal period was a sensitive window for the effects of PCBs
on growth [20]. Therefore, early detection of adverse factors affecting
the growth and development of adolescents and effective interventions
is crucial. Several studies have indicated that exposure to VOCs can
affect lung growth and development, leading to compromised respira-
tory health in infancy [21,22]. However, there is still a lack of evidence
regarding the effects of VOCs for adolescent growth.
Studying the mechanism of VOCs exposure on adolescent growth and
development is crucial for developing effective prevention strategies. It is
well-known that the accumulation of bone mineral density (BMD) is
essential for growth and development. Adolescence is an essential period
of bone development and maturity [23], and BMD continues to increase
during this stage, reaching about 90 % of peak bone mass in late adoles-
cence [24]. Existing studies have shown that there is a direct correlation
between BMD and growth indicators such as height and weight [25,26].
Moreover, VOCs can affect BMD levels by affecting the activity of bone
marrow cells, osteoblasts, and osteoclasts [27,28]. Under physiological
conditions, osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes collaborate to regulate
the balance between bone resorption and bone formation, thereby facili-
tating the maintenance of regular bone development [29]. According to
the study of Zhou et al. [30], among the VOCs that were related to BMD,
N-acetyl-S-(N-methylcarbamoyl)-L-cysteine (AMCC), Mandelic acid (MA),

speculate that BMD, such as total BMD, subtotal BMD and lumbar spine
BMD, may play a mediating role in the association between VOCs and
growth indicators. To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated the
mediation effects of BMD on the relationship between VOCs and growth
indicators.
Vitamin D, a fat-soluble micronutrient, has various and important
health benefits [31,32]. Some studies have shown that vitamin D defi-
ciency can impair bone homeostasis [33,34]. And short stature in chil-

yielded the exact opposite results. A meta-analysis of randomized
controlled trials showed that vitamin D supplementation had little to no
effect on linear growth in children [37]. Trilok-Kumar et al. [38] found
that early vitamin D supplementation had no long-term effects on body
composition and bone health. And Chowdhury et al. [39] also indicated
that vitamin D status in early childhood was not associated with linear
growth. Thus, the role of vitamin D in preventing the adverse effects of
VOCs exposure on adolescent growth deserves further investigation.
In this study, we used a combination of epidemiological and func-
tional enrichment analysis to explore the impact of VOCs exposure on
adolescent growth indicators and its potential mechanisms, and pro-
posing possible intervention strategies based on the mechanisms. Given
that the measures taken to contain coronavirus disease 2019 may affect
the correlation between VOCs exposure and growth indicators [40,41].
Also, the latest cycle of National Health and Nutrition Examination
Survey (NHANES) data lacked BMD and serum vitamin D information.
Therefore, we ultimately selected NHANES 2011–2018 for analysis.
First, we used weighted generalized linear models (GLM), weighted
quantile sum (WQS) and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR)
models to explore the relationship between VOCs exposure and
adolescent growth indicators. GLM is a common regression analysis
method used to analyze the association between a single component and
health outcomes. WQS and BKMR models are two popular methods for
assessing the combined effects of mixtures. Secondly, causal mediation
analysis and network pharmacological analysis were used to explore the
potential mechanism between VOCs exposure and adolescent growth
indicators. Finally, we attempted to explore whether dietary intake of
vitamin D can effectively prevent the adverse effects of VOCs exposure
on adolescent growth indicators.

2.2. Urinary VOC metabolites measurement
The collected urine samples were stored at −20 ℃until they were
shipped to the Division of Laboratory Sciences, National Center for

2.3. Body measurements
Body measurements, such as standing height, waist circumference,
and weight, were obtained by health technicians at the Mobile Exami-
nation Center for each participant. Weight was measured using a digital
scale and readings were recorded in kg. All health technicians were
professionally trained. The equipment calibration was performed by
health technicians. Detailed body measurement protocols, equipment,
and quality control instructions can be found in the NHANES anthro-
pometry
procedures
manual
(https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nh
anes/2017–2018/manuals/2017_Anthropometry_Procedures_Manual.
pdf). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms
divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Height-for-age and
BMI-for-age are identified as common growth indicators for adolescents
by the World Health Organization. For this purpose, we also calculated
the height-for-age and BMI-for-age of each participant using Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention growth charts for ages 12–19 years [46].
2.4. BMD measurements
Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is widely used for BMD
measurement because of its simplicity, speed and minimal radiation
exposure [47]. All eligible participants completed this measurement by
experienced, trained and certified professional radiology technologists,
while data of total BMD, subtotal BMD and lumbar spine BMD were
collected. The detailed DXA measurements of each part to obtain in-
formation is available at http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.
2.5. Vitamin D intake assessments
The NHANES database obtained the dietary intake of each partici-
pant through a 24-hour dietary recall. Energy and nutrient composition
values were calculated using the US Department of Agriculture Dietary
Sources of Nutrients database [48]. In this study, total daily intake of
vitamin D was obtained by combining the first 24-hour dietary recall
interviews to the daily intake of dietary supplements.
2.6. Serum vitamin D measurements
Briefly, the concentration of 25(OH)D in serum was detected by
liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). In this
study, the total serum 25 (OH)D was the result of adding the serum
concentrations of 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3. Based on the total serum 25
(OH)D concentrations, we defined participants with serum vitamin D
content less than 50 nmol/L as vitamin D deficiency group, and partic-
ipants with serum vitamin D content not less than 50 nmol/L as vitamin
D sufficiency group [49].
2.7. Covariates
Based on previous literature and the results of directed acyclic graph
(DAG) analysis [50,51], we adjusted several variables as covariates to
consider the potential confounders in our study: age, sex, race (Mexican
American, other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black and
other), smokers in the household, energy, physical activity (inactive or
active), and PIR (Fig. S2). A household member who smokes more than
or equal to one person was considered exposed to second-hand smoke.
Based on the duration of moderate to vigorous physical activity per
week, participants were classified as inactive (<10 min/week) and
active (>10 min/week) [52].
2.8. Network pharmacological analysis
The candidate targets of VOCs were predicted using Similarity
Ensemble Approach (SEA, http://sea.bkslab.org/) and PharmMapper
(http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html).

2.9. Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistics were performed on baseline features, where
continuous variables were expressed as either mean (standard deviation,
SD) or median (Q1, Q3), and categorical variables were represented by
frequency (percentage, %). We performed natural logarithm (ln) trans-
formations for 4 growth indicators (standing height, body weight, BMI,
waist circumference) and urinary VOCs to better fit the normal distri-
butions. Pearson’s correlation was used to examine the correlation be-
tween multiple VOCs. For weighted analysis, we utilized the provided
subsample weights from the VOCs data to better represent the true
population of the US.
Firstly, we conducted weighted GLM to investigate the relationship
between a single VOC exposure and growth indicators (standing height,
weight, BMI, waist circumference, height-for-age and BMI-for-age). To

minimize potential collinearity of variables, the least absolute shrinkage
and selection operator (LASSO) regression model was utilized. LASSO is
a feature selection method that supports variable selection by penalizing
the absolute values of the regression coefficients [44]. Through 10-fold
cross-validation to select the penalty parameter λ, 1000 iterations were
run to screen for key VOCs associated with growth indicators, aiming to
minimize mean squared error. After screening several key VOCs asso-
ciated with growth indicators, WQS regression and BKMR models were
used to evaluate the impact of mixed exposure to VOCs on growth in-
dicators. The models were adjusted for confounding factors as
mentioned above. In the WQS model, the data was divided into training
and testing sets in a 4:6 ratio and the model was established after
bootstrapping with 2000 iterations. When the WQS index was signifi-
cant, the respective weights were examined to determine the relative
contributions of each VOC to the growth indicators within the index.
However, WQS regression cannot evaluate the nonlinearity and in-
teractions of mixtures [53,54]. Therefore, BKMR model was used to
further analyze the mixed effects of VOCs on growth indicators. Spe-
cifically, the mixed effects of VOCs on growth indicators by comparing
growth indicator estimates per 10th percentile increase/decrease from
the median concentration (reference) of VOCs. The impact of each VOC
on various growth indicators was measured by calculating posterior
inclusion probabilities (PIPs). The PIPs quantify the relative importance
of each exposure in the model, utilizing a scale from 0 to 1, where values
nearer to 0 denote a lesser influence on the outcome. We used a
threshold of 0.5 to determine the significance of the contribution.
Moreover, both univariate and bivariate exposure-response functions
were utilized to evaluate the individual effect and interaction of VOCs.
In the BKMR model, we performed 10,000 iterations using the Markov
Chain Monte Carlo method for all analyses.
Additionally, we analyzed the role of BMD in the relationship be-
tween VOC mixtures and growth indicators by conducting a mediation
analysis. To effectively assess the growth and development of adoles-
cents, we considered three representative measures of BMD (total BMD,
subtotal BMD and lumbar spine BMD) to explore whether they mediated
the association between VOC mixtures and growth indicators [55]. We
initially employed three statistical models (GLM, WQS, and BKMR) to
analyze the relationship between VOCs exposure and BMD. Subse-
quently, we used non-parametric bootstrapping (n = 1000) for the
mediation analysis, assuming that the exposure-response relationship
between VOCs (X) and growth indicators (Y) is mediated by BMD (M).
This allowed us to explore the direct and indirect relationships as well as
the degree of the mediating effect, and further evaluate the proportion of
mediation (%) of BMD in the relationship between VOC mixtures and
growth indicators.
We stratified the population by sex, race, PIR, smokers in the
household, and physical activity to assess sensitivity and interactions in
different subgroups. Besides, we conducted several sensitivity analyses
to ensure the results of the relationship between VOCs and growth in-
dicators. Firstly, we utilized principal component analysis (PCA) to
evaluate the relationship between VOCs mixtures and growth indicators.
By constructing an environmental risk score (ERS) using the first eight
principal components and weighting them by variance contribution,
which together explained over 80 % of the total variance [44]. Secondly,
we used the baseline levels of urinary VOC metabolites as the exposure
level without adjusting for urine creatinine, but included it as a covar-
iate in fitting a GLM. Thirdly, we repeated the correlation analysis after
excluding individuals with excessively dilute (Cr < 30 mg/dL) or
concentrated (Cr > 300 mg/dL) urine samples. Fourthly, we included
serum vitamin D levels as covariates in fitting the GLM. Finally, we
employed the quantile g-computation (QGC) method to assess the
relationship between VOCs mixtures and growth indicators, which
combined the simplicity of weighted quantile regression with the flex-
ibility of g-computation to estimate the overall exposure effect of
increasing a quantile [56].
Finally, we conducted WQS regression to investigate the relationship
between VOCs mixtures and growth indicators in individuals with
different serum vitamin D levels (vitamin D deficiency or vitamin D
sufficiency). Weighted GLM was used to further explore the correlation
between dietary vitamin D intake and BMD and growth indicators.
All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1).
Weighted GLM, LASSO regression, WQS regression, BKMR, PCA, QGC
method, and mediation analyses were conducted using the R packages
‘survey’, ‘glmnet’, ‘gWQS’, ‘bkmr’, ‘FactoMineR’, ‘qgcomp’, and ‘medi-
ation’, respectively. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statis-
tically significant.

3.2. Relationship of VOCs with growth indicators
The weighted GLM was used to analyze the relationship between 15
VOCs and growth indicators (standing height, weight, BMI, waist
circumference, height-for-age and BMI-for-age). After adjusting for all
confounding factors, we found that ATCA, DHBMA, 3HPMA, MA,
MHBMA3 and PGA were negatively correlated with standing height.
AAMA, ATCA, CYMA, DHBMA, 3HPMA, HPMMA, MA, MHBMA3 and
PGA showed negatively association with weight. There was a signifi-
cantly negatively connection between AAMA, DHBMA, HPMMA,
MHBMA3, PGA and BMI. DHBMA, HPMMA, MHBMA3 and PGA were
negatively correlated with waist circumference. AAMA, CYMA, DHBMA,
HPMMA, MHBMA3 and PGA showed a significant negatively correlation
with BMI-for-age (all P-value < 0.05). However, the GLM did not reveal
a significant association between single urinary VOC and height-for-age
(all P-value > 0.05) (Table 2 and Table S2).
The results of Pearson correlation showed a positive correlation be-
tween the concentrations of multiple VOCs in urine. To reduce the
possible collinear variables in VOCs, we used an adaptive LASSO
penalized regression model to screen VOCs that were stronger correla-
tion with the growth indicators. Fig. 1 indicated the results of mean-
squared error associated with the penalty parameter (λ). The optimal
log-transformed λ values of standing height, weight, BMI, waist
circumference, height-for-age, and BMI-for-age were −6.363, −4.877,
−5.834, −4.719, −3.676 and −2.978, respectively. For standing height,
a total of 7 VOCs were selected, such as 3MHA+ 4MHA, AMCC, ATCA,
BMA, CEMA, CYMA and PGA. For weight, 3MHA+ 4MHA, ATCA,
CEMA, CYMA, DHBMA, HPMMA, MHBMA3 and PAG were included in
the analysis. For BMI, the results included the remaining 12 VOCs in
addition to 2MHA, AAMA, and 2HPMA. For waist circumference, a total
of 3MHA+ 4MHA, ATCA, HPMMA and PAG were screened. For height-
for-age, the analysis included 3MHA+ 4MHA, CEMA, CYMA, DHBMA,
HPMMA, and MA. For BMI-for-age, the results included 5 VOCs such as
3MHA+ 4MHA, BMA, DHBMA, HPMMA and PGA (Fig. S5).
To assess the mixed effects of VOCs on growth indicators, we per-
formed WQS regression analysis on the variables screened by LASSO
regression. After adjusting for potential confounders, we found that
WQS indices were significantly negatively associated with standing
height (β: −0.006, 95 % CI: −0.008, −0.004), weight (β: −0.023, 95 %
CI: −0.037, −0.010), BMI (β: −0.016, 95 % CI: −0.027, −0.004), waist
circumference (β: −0.010, 95 % CI: −0.018, −0.002), and BMI-for-age
(β: −0.084, 95 % CI: −0.147, −0.021) (Table S3). As shown in Fig. 2,
for weight, BMI, and BMI-for-age, DHBMA was the highest WQS weigh
among various VOCs. However, for standing height and waist circum-
ference, the highest contributions were made by ATAC (0.465) and PGA
(0.386), respectively.
After the suitable variables were selected by LASSO regression, the
effects of VOCs mixture on growth indicators were investigated by
BKMR model. As shown in Fig. 3, when fixed at the median concentra-
tion with VOCs, VOCs mixture concentration was negatively correlated
with standing height, weight, BMI, waist circumference, and BMI-for-
age. Table S4 summarizes the PIP results of each VOCs in the BKMR
model. HPMMA contributed the most to the overall association of VOCs
mixtures with weight (0.858), BMI (0.872), and BMI-for-age (0.994).
However, for the overall association between VOCs mixtures and
standing height and waist circumference, none of the VOCs had PIP
greater than 0.5. To determine the effect of a single VOC on growth
indicators, we fixed the concentrations of other VOCs at median levels

We further evaluated whether BMD mediated the negative rela-
tionship between VOCs mixtures and growth indicators. And we found

mixture, respectively. However, BMD had no significant mediating ef-

4. Discussion
In this cross-sectional study of US adolescents, we found a significant
negative association between VOCs exposure and growth indicators
(standing height, weight, BMI, waist circumference, and BMI-for-age),
and the BMD levels mediated the association between VOCs mixture
and growth indicators. The results of functional enrichment analysis
suggested that VOCs may be related to adolescent growth and devel-
opment through underlying biological mechanisms, and the most
important module of this result was significantly related to the IL-17
signaling pathway. In addition, VOCs may also act on the growth and
development of adolescents by targeting proteins such as IL-1β, CASP 3,
HSP90AA1, SRC and FOS. The association between dietary vitamin D
and BMD and growth indicators indicated that dietary vitamin D intake
may be a feasible strategy to prevent the adverse effects of VOCs
exposure on adolescent growth.
In recent years, with the rapid development of industrialization,
environmental pollution has become one of the main causes of human
diseases [57]. Existing findings suggested that exposure to air pollutants
may delay growth in children and adolescents. A Polish study showed
that the average stature of children and adolescents decreased as the
level of air pollution in the place where they live increased [58]. The
growth rate of children height living in areas with high pollution was
reduced by an average of 1.5 cm compared with children living in areas
with low pollution [59]. As widely exposed air pollutants in daily life,
VOCs come from a variety of sources (such as tobacco smoke, building
materials,
furniture,
and
cosmetics)
[60].
With
the
continuous
improvement of outdoor air quality, the adverse effects of indoor air
pollution on health have attracted wide attention. Studies showed that
people in developed countries spent up to 90 % of their time indoors
[61]. As the most common indoor air pollutant, the health risks of VOCs
cannot be ignored. Thus, a comprehensive assessment of the harm of
VOCs exposure to adolescent growth was of great public health signifi-
cance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate both single
and overall associations between VOCs exposure and adolescent growth,
while providing new insights into the mechanisms by which VOCs
exposure affects adolescent growth. In addition, our findings may fill
gaps in the understanding of the hazards of exposure to VOCs to human
health.
At present, there are few studies on VOCs exposure and growth. An
epidemiological study conducted in the US showed that exposed to
trichloroethylene was associated with low birth weight and fetal growth
restriction [62]. The results of another study showed that the exposure
of prenatal VOCs was associated with a reduction in neonatal weight
[63]. An experimental animal study also demonstrated that prenatal
exposure to styrene had effect on the growth and development of their
offspring [64]. Current research mostly focuses on single VOC compo-
nents and the adverse effects of VOCs on newborns, and there is
currently a lack of research evidence on the impact of VOCs exposure
with adolescent growth and development. In our study, for a single VOC,
we found that 1, 3-butadiene metabolites (DHBMA and MHBMA3),
ethylbenzene and styrene metabolite (PGA) were negatively associated
with growth indicators (standing height, weight, BMI, waist circumfer-
ence, BMI-for-age). In addition, we also found that DHBMA caused the
highest degree of adverse effects on growth indicators, which may be
related to the highest concentration it exhibited in all participants. In
stratified analysis, sex markedly modified the association between VOCs
and growth indicators (P for interaction <0.05). The results showed that
male growth and development during adolescence were more prone to
be affected by VOCs exposure than female. Although the exact mecha-
nism is still unclear, we can consider several possible hypotheses. Firstly,
the female immune system is evolutionarily capable of responding more
forcefully to the threat of infection [65]. Casimir et al. [66] found that
males produced more inflammatory factors after being stimulated by
mitogen monocytes. Secondly, males undergo later pubertal develop-
ment than females [67]. Therefore, exposure to VOCs in male adoles-
cents deserves special attention.

their daily lives, research that only focuses on a single VOC component
may cause some degree of bias [68]. Therefore, we conducted a

models consistently indicated combined VOCs exposure was inversely

important implications for promoting health. Interestingly, in the WQS
model, the 1, 3-butadiene metabolite DHBMA was the main contributor
to the adverse effects of VOCs on growth indicators (weight, BMI, and
BMI-for-age). 1, 3-butadiene is ubiquitous in the environment. Due to
the significant potential threat to human health, the Occupational Safety
and Health Administration has recommended that occupational expo-

the DHBMA remained the main contributor to the adverse effects of
VOCs on BMI-for-age. However, crotonaldehyde metabolite HPMMA
was a major contributor to more affected growth indicators, such as
weight, BMI, and BMI-for-age. The reason for this difference is mainly
attributed to the different estimation methods of variable importance. In
addition, in the WQS model, HPMMA also ranked prominently in the
ranking of weighted contribution. At the same time, our study found that
both DHBMA and HPMMA were at relatively high levels in the urine of
US adolescents. Therefore, those adverse effects on the growth of ado-
lescents deserve attention. Our research findings emphasized the ne-
cessity of implementing interventions to reduce VOCs exposure during
adolescence, such as increasing indoor ventilation, the selection of
low-emitting products and installing an air purifier.
In this study, we found that VOCs were negatively correlated with
BMD, and BMD was positively correlated with growth indicators.
Similar to our results, a cross-sectional study of NHANES suggested that
long-term exposure to chloroform may lead to a reduction in BMD [28].
Zanlorenci et al. ’s study showed that BMD was directly related to
growth indicators [70]. Based on the above research results, we surmise
that BMD might play a significant mediation effect in the associations of
exposure to VOCs with growth indicators. The results showed that BMD
had a significant mediating role in the association between VOCs
exposure and adolescent growth, and the mediated proportion ranging
from 4.3 % to 53.4 %. However, the mechanism by which BMD mediates
the adverse effects of VOCs exposure on growth remains unclear.
Therefore, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses for the
overlapping targets between VOCs targets and adolescent growth and
development proteins. The results showed overlapping targets were
significantly enriched in IL-17 signaling pathways. Previous studies have
shown that the IL-17 signaling pathway is closely related to bone health
and growth and development in children and adolescents [71–73].
IL-17, as a pleiotropic factor produced by Th17 T cells, can induce excess
inflammation through cytokine cascade [74]. It has been reported that
treatment with anti-IL-17 antibodies or silencing the IL-17 signaling
pathway can reduce joint inflammation and articular bone erosion [75,
76]. This result may be achieved by reducing bone loss by inhibiting
RANKL expression on osteoblasts or stimulating the up-regulation of
regulatory cytokines such as IL-4, IL-12 and gamma-interferon. Also, the
increase of inflammatory factors also has adverse effects on growth and
development [77]. One study in newborns showed that IL-17 may affect
pre-eclampsia related fetal growth restriction [73]. Another study also
showed that air pollutants may lead to osteoclast production by inducing
inflammatory factors [78]. Therefore, VOCs exposure may affect BMD
by activating the IL-17 signaling pathway, thereby having adverse ef-
fects on the growth and development of adolescents.
Our findings also revealed the role of dietary vitamin D on BMD and
growth indicators. VOCs exposure has been reported to be associated
with a decrease in BMD [30]. At the same time, multiple studies have
shown that exposure to air pollutants during pregnancy is detrimental to
fetal growth [79,80]. For children and adolescents, several epidemio-
logical studies have shown that vitamin D intake is beneficial for their
growth and bone development [36,81]. Interestingly, there were also
several studies demonstrating that vitamin D supplementation had no
effect on the bone health and growth and development of children and
adolescents [82–84]. Our results showed that the adverse effects of
VOCs on adolescents with low serum vitamin D levels were significantly
greater than those with high serum vitamin D levels. This result sug-
gested that dietary intake of vitamin D may alleviate the impact of VOCs
on adolescent growth. Therefore, we further investigated the effect of
dietary vitamin D intake on growth indicators in adolescents, and found
that there was a positive correlation between vitamin D intake and BMD
and growth indicators in adolescents. The above results further
emphasized the beneficial effects of dietary supplementation of vitamin
D on the growth and development of adolescents, especially those with
high levels of VOCs in urine. In addition to obtaining vitamin D through
dietary sources, sun exposure has long been considered essential for
maintaining a sufficient vitamin D status [85]. Therefore, supplement-
ing with vitamin D and increasing their basking activity may be two
effective ways to prevent the adverse effects of VOCs exposure on
adolescent growth and development.
Our study has several advantages. Firstly, this is the first study to
comprehensively explore the effects of single and mixed VOCs exposure
on adolescent growth. At the same time, we found the mediating role of
BMD in VOCs exposure and adolescent growth. This provided more
comprehensive evidence of the adverse effects of VOCs on adolescent
growth. Secondly, we further explored the potential mechanism of the
adverse effects of VOCs on adolescent growth by using the method of
network pharmacology analysis, and found that VOCs may lead to the
activation of IL-17 signaling pathway. In addition, we also found that
dietary supplementation of vitamin D may be a feasible way to prevent
the adverse effects of VOCs exposure on growth. Finally, we performed
various stratification analyses and sensitivity analyses to demonstrate
the robustness of our findings. Nonetheless, there are some limitations to
this study. First, this is a cross-sectional study, and we can’t make causal
inferences about the relationship between VOCs and adolescent growth.
Second, despite adjusting for some covariates, we still cannot exclude
the possibility of other potentially unmeasured confounders having an
impact on this study, such as the participants’ occupational exposure,
disease states and medication use. Thirdly, single-point urinary samples

5. Conclusion
In summary, our results suggested that exposure to single VOC and
mixed VOCs may affect adolescent growth through three statistical
models. 1, 3-butadiene and crotonaldehyde were identified as the main
contributors due to their higher weights and the PIPs. The BMD played
an important mediating role in the adverse effects of VOCs on adolescent
growth. According to the results of PPI and functional enrichment
analysis, we believed that the IL-17 signaling pathway may be the po-
tential mechanism of VOCs exposure affecting adolescent growth.
Additionally, the attenuation of this association by elevated serum
vitamin D and the positive correlation between vitamin D and growth
indicators indicated that dietary supplementation of vitamin D may be a
viable strategy to prevent the adverse effects of VOCs exposure on
adolescent growth.
Environmental implication
Volatile organic compounds (VOCs), a group of pollutants pervasive
in daily life, are absorbed by the human body in various ways, resulting
in the occurrence of various diseases. The results of this study suggested
that VOCs exposure may affect adolescent growth, with bone mineral
density playing an important mediating role, and IL-17 signaling
pathway was the potential mechanism. Vitamin D may be a potential
intervention strategy to prevent the adverse effects of VOCs on adoles-
cent growth. Our results not only revealed the potential relationship and
mechanism between VOCs exposure and adolescent growth, but also
provided a potential intervention strategy.
