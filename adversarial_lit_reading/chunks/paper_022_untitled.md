Effects of high ambient temperatures on liver function in an
occupational population: Examining the mediation role of
inﬂammatory cells and moderation role of age factors
Mengqing Yan1,2,3, Bin Yang3,4, Menghan Li3, Wenjun Kang5, Yongli Yang6 and Wei Wang3✉
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer Nature America, Inc. 2026
BACKGROUND: Occupational exposure to high ambient temperature may affect liver function, but pathways remain unclear.
OBJECTIVE: To examine associations between high ambient temperature and liver enzymes among steelworkers, and to evaluate
mediation by inﬂammatory cells and moderation by age.
METHODS: In this cross-sectional study of 1111 steelworkers, high ambient temperature exposure was classiﬁed at the workshop
level using wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) per Chinese standards (GBZ 2.2-2007): time-weighted average WBGT ≥25 °C (high-
temperature) vs. <25 °C (control). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and inﬂammatory cell counts
were measured from venous blood. Multivariable generalized linear models estimated associations; mediation (via lymphocytes)
used bootstrap conﬁdence intervals; age moderation was tested.
RESULTS: Compared with controls, the high ambient temperature exposure group exhibited elevated levels of ALT and AST, as well
as higher counts of white blood cells, lymphocytes, monocytes, and eosinophils (P < 0.05). High ambient temperature exposure
emerged as independently associated with ALT (β = 0.384, 95%CI: 0.222, 0.546) and AST (β = 0.238, 95%CI:0.065, 0.410) levels.
Lymphocyte counts statistically mediated the relationship between high ambient temperature exposure and elevated serum ALT
(β = 0.062, 95%CI: 0.026, 0.104) and AST (β = 0.046, 95%CI:0.009, 0.092) levels. Additionally, age only moderated the path between
high ambient temperature exposure and lymphocyte count (β = -0.192, 95%CI: -0.316, -0.067).
SIGNIFICANCE: Occupational heat was associated with higher ALT/AST; lymphocyte-related indirect association was observed and
attenuated with age. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to assess causality.
IMPACT: Findings support WBGT-based heat monitoring, routine ALT and AST screening in hot posts, and targeted acclimatization
hydration and work- rest protocols, especially for younger workers, while future studies test whether enhanced heat controls
improve liver outcomes.
Keywords: High ambient temperature exposure; Liver function; Inﬂammatory cells; Mediating effects; Moderating effects

INTRODUCTION
Globally, a substantial proportion of the workforce is exposed to
occupational heat stress, a major public health concern that is
projected to worsen due to climate change. This issue is
particularly pressing in low- and middle-income countries, where
workers are often engaged in labor-intensive activities under
extreme heat conditions. The adverse effects of occupational heat
stress are well-documented, encompassing a range of health and
productivity outcomes [1]. The International Labour Organization
estimates that over 2.4 billion workers are exposed to excessive
heat annually [2]. Exposure to high ambient temperatures is
known to have detrimental effects on human health, contributing
to approximately 0.64% of annual deaths in China [3, 4]. It’s worth
noting that China boasts the world’s largest working population.
In 2021, the National Bureau of Statistics reported 74,652 million
occupational workers in China, accounting for 52.85% of the total
population. Occupational health concerns related to heat have
long persisted, especially in professions involving strenuous
physical labor or exposure to high ambient temperatures [5, 6].
Research evidence has indicated that long-term exposure to high
ambient temperatures can result in adaptive damage to various
bodily systems, including the circulatory system [7], nervous
system [8], and digestive system [9], and in severe cases, it may
lead to heatstroke and other related illnesses [10, 11]. While the

frequently emerges as a common complication of heatstroke (HS)
stemming from exposure to high ambient temperatures and is
often the direct cause of death in affected patients [18]. However,
much less is known about the potential subclinical effects of
chronic, occupational heat exposure at levels below those
required to cause heatstroke. Animal experimental studies have
shown that long-term exposure to high temperatures can cause
damage to liver tissue: Gupta et al. used Sprague Dawley (SD) rats
as a model to expose them to moderate but sustained heat stress
and found that compared with control rats, The liver function test
levels of rats in the heat exposure experimental group decreased,
the levels of free radicals and inﬂammatory factors increased, the
structural deterioration
of liver lobules increased, and the
mitochondrial structure was destroyed [19]. Most of the research
on the impact of high temperature on the liver in the population
focuses on the evaluation of the impact on the liver when high-
temperature work leads to more serious outcomes. Therefore, it is
imperative to investigate the effects of chronic high ambient
temperature exposure on these key liver function indices among
occupational workers. Such investigations contribute signiﬁcantly
to our understanding of heat-induced liver damage and the
development of targeted protective measures.
This study aimed to investigate the correlation between high
ambient temperatures and liver function indicators, speciﬁcally
ALT and AST levels, among workers at a central China steel plant.
Additionally, by analyzing the mediating and moderating effects
of high ambient temperature exposure on liver function, this
research provides valuable insights and directions for further
investigation into the mechanisms. These ﬁndings offer a basis
and theoretical framework for the effective prevention and
management of heat-induced liver effects.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study subjects
A total of 1111 participants were enrolled in this study. The high ambient
temperature exposure group comprised 606 participants, while the control
group, consisting of 505 individuals, worked in the same factory without
exposure to high ambient temperatures. Inclusion required age 18 – 60
years and ≥1 year of employment at the plant. Exclusion criteria were
documented organic diseases of major organs (e.g., liver or kidney disease)
or malignancy; pregnancy or lactation; and incomplete questionnaires/
records or missing key exposure or outcome data.
The research protocol received ethical approval from Zhengzhou
University’s ethics committee (ZZUIRB2021-153). Before participating, all
participants provided written informed consent.
High ambient temperature exposure assessment
High ambient temperatures were assessed using a comprehensive wet-
bulb globe temperature (WBGT) meter in accordance with the Occupa-
tional Health Standard of China GBZ/T 189.7—2007. Measurements
included dry-bulb, natural wet-bulb, and globe temperatures (via WBGT
meter, dry-bulb thermometer, natural wet-bulb thermometer, and black-
globe thermometer), from which the WBGT index was calculated per the
standard. For workplaces without productive heat sources, three ﬁxed
points were measured and averaged; for workplaces with heat sources, 3–5
points were averaged. Where areas differed in thermal or ventilation
conditions, two points per area were measured and averaged. For year-
round hot work, monitoring was conducted in the hottest summer month;
for intermittent hot work, in the hottest month of the project period; and
for outdoor work, on sunny days with solar radiation during the hottest
month. When the heat source was stable, measurements were taken three
times per day (≈0.5 h after start, mid-shift, and ≈0.5 h before end) and
averaged. When the heat source was unstable or cycles were long, a time-
weighted average (TWA) WBGT was calculated. Following GBZ 2.2—2007,
hot work is classiﬁed into four levels by workplace WBGT and exposure
duration. Consistent with the GBZ 2.2—2007 deﬁnition, workshops with
WBGT ≥25 °C were designated the high-temperature group, and those
with WBGT < 25 °C the control group.
Liver function measures
Participants were required to fast for a minimum of 8 h and abstain from
alcohol consumption for 24 h before providing approximately 5 ml of
peripheral blood. The blood was collected using EDTA anticoagulant tubes
and tubes containing coagulant. An automatic biochemical analyzer was
used to detect inﬂammatory cell counts, such as white blood cells,
lymphocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils, and liver
function indicators, speciﬁcally ALT and AST.
Variable selection
The questionnaire survey collected basic information, occupational history
(including occupational experience, occupational time, work unit, work
workshop, type of work, exposure factors, etc.), past medical history, acute
and chronic occupational disease history, family history, medication
history, etc, as well as height and weight. The ﬁnal multivariable models
adjusted for sex, employment duration (years), smoking status, alcohol use,
and co-exposures measured in the workplace: noise (dBA), total dust (mg/
m³), and carbon monoxide (mg/m³). According to WHO guidelines, current
smokers are deﬁned as individuals who smoke daily for more than a year,
and current drinkers are deﬁned as people who drink at least twice a week
for at least six months [20].
Statistical analysis
The physical examination data were entered in a double-blind manner
using EpiData 3.0 and analyzed using SPSS 25.0. The median and
interquartile range were used to describe measurement data that did not
follow a normal distribution, while the count value and count percentage (n
(%)) were used for the statistical description of the count data. Differences
in liver function indicator levels were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U
test, while the χ2 test was used to compare the differences in rates between
groups.

DISCUSSION
In this cross-sectional study of steelworkers, high ambient
temperature which classiﬁed using weighted wet-bulb globe
temperature (WBGT) at the workshop was associated with higher
serum ALT and AST and with higher white blood cell, lymphocyte,
monocyte, and eosinophil counts. Furthermore, lymphocyte
counts were identiﬁed as mediators in this association, while
age acted as a modiﬁer of the association between high ambient
temperature exposure and lymphocyte counts. Our results are
consistent with occupational evidence that heat exposure relates
to hepatic biomarkers where liver injury is frequently observed. In
a study on ﬁreﬁghters, exposure to ﬁre emissions and heat was
associated with signiﬁcantly altered values of biomarkers related
to liver injury, indicating the potential impact of their occupational
exposure on liver health [22].Solar ultraviolet radiation and heat
stress are major concerns for outdoor workers. Heat stroke, the
most severe form of heat stress, can cause irreversible damage to
multiple organs including the liver [23]. Our ﬁndings align with
prior research, demonstrating a positive association between high
ambient temperature exposure and ALT and AST levels. Impor-
tantly, our ﬁndings address subclinical, chronic occupational heat
below HS thresholds in an industrial setting, complementing the
stronger evidence base for liver damage outcomes. Recent studies
have delved into the mechanisms of liver damage caused by high
ambient temperature exposure. Heat stress triggers a cascade of
physiological and metabolic reactions in response to extreme
temperatures, such as those experienced during high ambient
temperature exposure [24]. The liver is a primary target organ
affected by heat stress damage, resulting in protein inactivation
and structural damage in liver cells [25]. These changes are
reﬂected in early increases in serum total bilirubin, alanine
aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and other signaling
factors [26]. Prolonged heat stress can cause or aggravate cell
damage or dysfunction [27]. The liver, rich in blood ﬂow,
undergoes redistribution of blood during heat stress, leading to
decreased blood ﬂow through the liver [28]. This aggravates liver
cell hypoxia, activates proteases, and triggers liver macrophages.
This cascade of events damages mitochondrial structures reduces
superoxide dismutase synthesis, and increases the production and
accumulation of oxygen free radicals, resulting in aggravated
oxidative damage to the liver [29, 30].
Lymphocyte count emerged as a mediator in the relationship
between high ambient temperature exposure and liver function
indexes, speciﬁcally ALT and AST. This ﬁnding suggests that high
ambient temperature exposure may lead to changes in lympho-
cyte counts, subsequently inﬂuencing ALT and AST levels.
Changes in lymphocyte counts are often associated with chronic
inﬂammatory reactions or viral infections [31], consistent with the
hypothesis that steel mill workers, exposed to long-term high-
temperature environments, experience chronic liver inﬂammation.

In contrast, other inﬂammatory cells like neutrophils, are typically
associated with acute Inﬂammation [32, 33], while eosinophils are
more common in parasitic [34] or allergic diseases such as asthma
[35]. These cells did not signiﬁcantly play a mediating role in the
association between high ambient temperature exposure and liver
function indicators, which was consistent with our ﬁndings. As
previously mentioned, the human body experiences heat stress
when exposed to high ambient temperature exposure, leading to
a series of adaptive [36], stress, and immune-inﬂammatory
responses as it compensates for heat-induced stress [37]. Notably,
the inﬂammatory response constitutes a vital component of this
compensatory process. This inﬂammatory response activates
lymphocytes, leading to an increase in their numbers within the
body [38]. We emphasize that these mechanistic claims derive
from prior literature rather than direct measurements in our
dataset; in our study, the statistical indirect association via
lymphocytes is consistent with, but does not prove, an immune-
mediated pathway.
Finally, our mediation analyses demonstrated that age primarily
regulates the pathway linking high ambient temperature exposure
to increased lymphocyte counts, such that the indirect effect on
ALT/AST via lymphocytes is stronger in younger workers and
attenuates with age. Given the cross-sectional design, mediation
reﬂects a statistical decomposition (total = direct + indirect
association) and does not establish temporality or causality. The
age interaction implies heterogeneity of the association: younger
workers showed stronger exposure-related lymphocyte differ-
ences. In light of the ﬁndings discussed above, this study suggests
that older individuals in the exposed group may exhibit improved
adaptation or acclimatization to high ambient temperatures,
thereby enhancing their ability to manage the inﬂammatory
response induced by such conditions, ultimately reducing liver
damage. This pattern suggests two, non-mutually exclusive
explanations.

In summary, this study focused on steel mill workers as its
research subjects to investigate the correlation between high
ambient temperature exposure and the levels of liver function
indicators, speciﬁcally ALT and AST. Additionally, we explored the
mediating role of inﬂammatory cells and the moderating role of
age in the context of high ambient temperature exposure
affecting ALT and AST levels. While previous research primarily
relied on animal experiments to assess the effects of high ambient
temperature exposure on liver function, our study concentrated
on occupational workers exposed to these conditions, rendering
the conclusions more applicable at the population level. None-
theless, this study has certain limitations. As discussed, cross-
sectional design precludes causal inference between occupational
high ambient temperature exposure and serum liver enzyme
levels. Second, despite multivariable adjustment, residual con-
founding could not be excluded; important covariates such as
hydration status, BMI, medication use (including potentially
hepatotoxic drugs), and other factors affecting the liver were
not obtained. Third, ALT/AST and inﬂammatory cell counts were
measured once, which may not capture short-term biological
variability. Fourth, high ambient temperature is not a continuous
exposure and varies by task, shift, and day, which may bias the
association toward or away from the null hypothesis. In addition,
our exposure deﬁnition followed GBZ thresholds to align with
local regulation and occupational practice, increasing the policy
relevance of our ﬁndings for Chinese workplaces. We acknowl-

that occupational exposure to high ambient temperatures is
associated with higher serum ALT and AST and seems to act, at
least in part, through increased lymphocyte counts; this effect
appears stronger in younger workers and attenuates with age.
Based on these ﬁndings, routine liver function screening in hot
jobs, targeted heat-health promotion for younger workers, and
the integration of liver risk indicators into occupational heat-
health guidelines seem warranted.
