SUMMARY
Given the inconsistent evidence regarding hearing aid use and reduced dementia risk, this study evaluates 
whether hearing aid use, particularly effective use, is associated with lower probable dementia risk among 
hearing-impaired older adults. Using harmonized longitudinal data from 61,089 hearing-impaired partici­
pants (aged 55+ years) from seven cohorts (CHARLS, KLOSA, SHARE, ELSA, TILDA, HRS, and MHAS), we 
employ IPTW-corrected Cox models stratified by country income. Over a 6.5-year average follow-up 
(8,911 events), hearing aid use is associated with reduced dementia risk (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.91, 95% con­
fidence interval [CI] 0.88–0.94). This association is confined to participants reporting effective hearing 
improvement (HR = 0.86, 95% CI 0.80–0.93), whereas those reporting poor hearing improvement show no 
reduced risk (HR = 0.98, 95% CI 0.90–1.07). The association is stronger in middle-income countries (HR = 
0.76) and among subgroup populations, including those aged <70 years, women, those who are unmarried, 
and those with lower education. Quality hearing rehabilitation is a potential public health priority for address­
ing the dementia burden.
INTRODUCTION
By the year 2050, dementia is predicted to affect 150 million peo­
ple worldwide, contributing to 115.8 million disability-adjusted 
life years.1 Hearing loss, which affects approximately 10% of 
individuals aged 40–69 years, 30% over 65 years, and 70%– 
90% aged 85 years or older,2,3 has emerged as one of the 
most potentially modifiable risk factors for dementia.1 According 
to the 2024 Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, hearing 
loss accounts for 7% of dementia cases, making it one of the

This implies that managing hearing loss is a significant public 
health priority in the context of dementia risk.1 Hearing aids are 
generally considered the gold-standard first-line treatment for 
adults with hearing loss.4 Yet, more evidence is required to 
conclude whether hearing loss treatment using hearing aids 
could reduce the rate of cognitive decline in older adults with 
hearing loss.5
The ACHIEVE randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that 
hearing aid use was associated with a 48% reduction in 3-year 
cognitive decline among a higher-risk subgroup—individuals 
with increased vulnerability to cognitive decline due to factors 
such as lower baseline cognitive function, greater cardiovascular 
risk, and other dementia-related risk factors.6 While the trial’s 
authors suggested that longer follow-up might be necessary 
to detect benefits in a broader population of individuals with 
hearing loss, conducting such long-term RCTs presents signifi­
cant practical and ethical challenges.7 For instance, ethically 
justifying withholding hearing aids from control group partici­
pants over extended periods is difficult, given the known benefits 
of hearing rehabilitation, and participant crossover (where 
control participants independently obtain hearing aids) can 
undermine trial integrity. In light of these challenges, high-quality 
observational studies serve a critical complementary role 
by providing valuable insights into the potential long-term 
effects of hearing aid use on dementia risk in large, real-world 
populations.
However, evidence from observational studies remains limited 
and inconsistent.8–11 For instance, a French prospective study 
suggested that hearing aid use was associated with less cogni­
tive decline in older adults with hearing loss.12 A recent target 
trial conducted in Australia suggested that hearing aid might 
reduce dementia risk but could not change the cognitive 
scores.13 In contrast, other studies from the United States and 
the United Kingdom found no significant difference in dementia 
incidence between hearing aid users and non-users.9,14 These 
conflicting results may be due to methodological limitations 
such as small sample sizes9,12 and diverse study protocols. 
Another critical but often overlooked issue is the effectiveness 
of hearing aid use.15 Simply wearing a hearing aid does not guar­
antee effective rehabilitation: some users experience substantial 
improvement in hearing, while others benefit minimally.16 Vari­
ability in the effectiveness of hearing aid use may dilute the asso­
ciations between hearing aid use and cognitive outcomes, 
explaining the inconsistent results of previous studies.17 The 
methodological limitations of prior research, including small 
sample sizes, lack of generalizability, and failure to account for 
hearing aid effectiveness, highlight the need for a more rigorous 
approach. Therefore, a large-scale longitudinal analysis 
that pools harmonized data from multiple international cohorts 
is essential to increase the overall sample size, enhance 
generalizability, and, most importantly, achieve the statistical 
power needed to investigate the role of hearing aid effectiveness 
by analyzing smaller, refined subgroups.18 Furthermore, this 
approach enables the investigation of how the association be­
tween hearing aid use and dementia risk varies across different 
levels of national economic development, which may be a vital 
step toward tailoring effective global public health strategies.19
In this study, we conducted a large longitudinal analysis using 
harmonized data from seven aging cohort studies spanning 33 
countries. We examined whether older adults with hearing loss 
who use hearing aids, particularly those who report effective 
hearing improvement, have a lower risk of developing probable 
dementia compared to those who do not use hearing aid. We 
also investigated whether this association varies according to 
national economic development levels and performed subgroup 
analyses to identify populations for whom the use of hearing 
aids is most strongly associated with a lower risk of probable 
dementia.
RESULTS
Population characteristics
Our pooled dataset included 61,089 hearing-impaired older 
adults (mean age at baseline 69.4 ± 9.3 years; 49.4% women) 
(Table 1). The baseline characteristics by cohort are detailed in 
Table S12. Briefly, participants from middle-income countries 
were, on average, younger than those from high-income coun­
tries. Gender distribution was roughly balanced across income 
groups (women: 48.7%–51.2%). However, participants from 
middle-income countries had lower educational attainment (col­
lege education: 2.4% vs. 22.2%) and higher rates of marriage or 
partnership (77.7% vs. 67.7%) than those from high-income 
countries.
The prevalence of hearing aid use at baseline was low, with 
only 15.4% of hearing-impaired participants reporting using a 
hearing aid (Table 1). The adoption rates varied substantially 
across cohorts, ranging from as low as 0.9% in CHARLS to 
as high as 34.1% in TILDA (Table S12). When stratified by 
the country’s income level, hearing aid use was markedly lower 
in middle-income countries (2.6%) compared to high-income 
countries (20.0%). Overall, about two-thirds (63.5%) of hearing 
aid users reported good device effectiveness (Table 1). Rates 
varied between cohorts, ranging from 21.6% (CHARLS) to 
81.0% (KLOSA). By income level, high-income countries re­
ported higher effectiveness (64.4%) than middle-income coun­
tries (46.0%). Figure 1 maps hearing aid use and effectiveness 
by country: notably, Denmark had the highest hearing aid 
adoption rate, while China had the lowest; conversely, 
Switzerland had the highest effectiveness ratings, whereas 
China had the lowest.
After applying IPTW weighting, baseline characteristics (age, 
gender, education, etc.) were well balanced between hearing 
aid users and non-users, as well as between the poor effective­
ness and good effectiveness groups (Tables S13 and S14), sug­
gesting that our weighted analyses appropriately account for 
baseline differences.

Hearing aid effectiveness and probable dementia
The effectiveness of hearing aid strongly influenced the probable 
dementia risk. Figure 4 presents the HRs for probable dementia 
for good hearing aid effectiveness and poor hearing aid effec­
tiveness (hearing-impaired participants without hearing aids as 
the reference). Participants who used hearing aids and reported 
good hearing improvement experienced a markedly lower risk of 
probable dementia compared to those who did not use hearing 
aids. In the pooled analysis, the HR was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.80–0.93) 
for the good-effectiveness group compared to non-users. This 
corresponds to a roughly 14% lower risk of probable dementia 
risk. By contrast, hearing aid users who reported poor hearing 
improvement had no significant risk reduction—their HR for 
probable dementia was 0.98 (95% CI 0.90–1.07), essentially 
equivalent to that of the reference group of hearing-impaired 
non-users. In analyses stratified by country income level, the 
high-income countries had significant probable dementia risk 
reduction only among good-effectiveness users (HR 0.87, 95% 
CI 0.81–0.95), whereas middle-income countries exhibited pro­
tective associations for both good (HR 0.70, 95%CI 0.56–0.88) 
and poor effectiveness users (HR 0.70, 95%CI 0.55–0.90) 
(Figure 4). The E-values for the association between good 
hearing aid effectiveness and probable dementia were 1.45 
(harmonized cohort), 1.42 (high-income countries), and 1.89 
(middle-income countries). In cohort-specific analyses, while 
no significant association was found in any study for the poor 
effectiveness group, the good effectiveness group showed a 
statistically significant lower dementia risk in the KLOSA and 
HRS cohorts, with a consistent trend of lower risk (all HRs 
<1.0) across all seven studies (Figure S4).
Sensitivity analyses for the effectiveness findings were largely 
consistent with the primary analysis (Figures 4 and S5). When re­
stricting baseline age (≥60 years), requiring multiple follow-ups 
(≥2 waves), using dementia criteria without IQCODE or self- 
report data, excluding non-persistent users, or analyzing only 
complete cases without imputation, the good-effectiveness 
group consistently showed a protective association, while the 
poor-effectiveness group remained equivalent to non-users in 
terms of risk. The protective association for good-effectiveness 
users was also generally held when excluding the first 3 years 
of follow-up.
Subgroup and interaction analyses of hearing aid effective­
ness showed no significant interactions across most demo­
graphic subgroups (Figure 5). A significant interaction by SDI 
was observed in the harmonized cohort: effective hearing aid 
use was associated with a lower risk of probable dementia in 
high (HR = 0.76) and low (HR = 0.82) SDI regions but not in mid­
dle-SDI regions. In high-income countries, a significant interac­
tion was found only for family income, with a protective associa­
tion observed among individuals with lower family income 
(HR = 0.84).
DISCUSSION
In this large, harmonized analysis of seven longitudinal cohorts 
from 33 countries, we found that hearing aid use was associated 
with a lower risk of probable dementia in older adults with hear­
ing loss. The participants who used hearing aid had a 9% lower 
hazard of probable dementia compared to non-users. Crucially, 
the association between hearing aid and probable dementia ap­
peared to depend on their perceived effectiveness: participants 
who reported a substantial improvement in hearing with their 
hearing aid had about a 14% lower risk of probable dementia, 
whereas those who gained little benefit had no significant risk 
reduction. Our results also indicated heterogeneity by country in­
come level, with the association with a lower risk of probable de­
mentia appearing stronger in settings characterized by lower 
baseline hearing aid adoption, particularly in the middle-income 
countries. Subgroup analyses suggested that the association 
between hearing aid use and lower probable dementia risk 
was more pronounced in certain demographic groups, specif­
ically women, participants with lower educational attainment, 
and unmarried individuals. In summary, our findings highlight 
that effective hearing aid use, particularly in contexts with limited 
resources, is linked to a significantly lower risk of probable de­
mentia in later life.

Our findings may be interpreted in light of several hypotheses 
linking hearing loss and dementia. First, the sensory deficit hy­
pothesis suggested that reduced hearing stimulation might 
lead to decline in brain volume or concurrent declines in the 
auditory periphery and the central nervous system, which hinder 
general cognitive and emotional processes in favor of auditory 
perception.24 Effective hearing aid use might help restore audi­
tory stimulation before prolonged deprivation occurs, thereby 
potentially mitigating these neural changes and slowing cogni­
tive decline. Second, the cognitive load hypothesis suggested 
that individuals with hearing loss may allocate greater cognitive 
resources to effortful listening and speech perception, leaving 
fewer resources available for non-perceptual cognitive pro­
cesses.25 Successful rehabilitation could reduce the extra 
cognitive effort required to understand speech, allowing more 
cognitive resources to be redirected toward other cognitive 
functions.26 In addition, hearing loss has been associated with 
reduced social interaction and increased social isolation. Individ­
uals with hearing difficulties might communicate less frequently 
with family and friends, spent less time outside the home, and 
withdrew from social and leisure activities.27,28 These factors 
had been consistently linked to an increased risk of demen­
tia.29,30 By improving communication ability, effective hearing 
aid use might facilitate social participation and maintain social 
connectedness, which might in turn help reduce the risk of 
cognitive decline.31–33
Our subgroup and interaction analyses identified several fac­
tors that may modify the association between hearing aid use 
and probable dementia risk. In harmonized cohort, the protective 
association was more pronounced in regions with higher SDI and 
among specific demographic groups, including individuals aged 
<70 years, women, unmarried individuals, and those with lower 
education. Furthermore, this association was almost exclusively 
driven by the effectiveness of hearing aids. Classic mechanisms, 
such as improved communication and reduced social isolation, 
may partly explain the associations.34,35 In addition, within these 
subgroups, individuals who obtain and effectively use hearing 
aids may represent a particularly health-literate and proactive 
segment of the population,36 and these unmeasured character­
istics could themselves confound the observed association.37 At 
the contextual level, higher-SDI regions typically reflect more 
mature healthcare systems, including better access to audiolog­
ical services and professional device fitting, which may help 
maximize the effectiveness of hearing aids. Stratified analyses 
by country income level showed different patterns. In high-in­
come countries, the pattern was similar to that observed in 
harmonized cohort, whereas in middle-income countries, the as­
sociation was most pronounced in the high family income sub­
group. Access to hearing aids and related healthcare services 
is often more strongly shaped by personal economic resources. 
Consequently, hearing aid users in these contexts may represent 
a positively selected group with greater socioeconomic re­
sources, better healthcare access, and higher health literacy 
compared with the surrounding population.38 Notably, some re­
sults in middle-income countries did not follow the overall 
pattern. For example, hearing aid use was associated with a 
higher dementia risk in the high-SDI subgroup, and among 
women, good-effectiveness users showed a higher hazard ratio 
than poor-effectiveness users. These findings should be consid­
ered in the context of the small sample sizes in these specific 
subgroups, which may have resulted in limited statistical power 
and unstable point estimates. Moreover, the largely overlapping 
confidence intervals between compared groups suggest that 
these differences may not be statistically meaningful. Caution 
is therefore warranted when interpreting these results.
Beyond the individual-level factors discussed, the notable var­
iations in hearing aid usage, effectiveness, and the associated 
lower probable risk of dementia that we observed across country 
income levels likely stem from fundamental disparities in health­
care system architecture and socioeconomic realities. In high-in­
come countries, where access barriers are relatively low due to 
public or private insurance systems, the selection process may 
be more inclined to screen for individuals who not only seek 
but also pursue effective intervention.39 This explanatory path 
aligns with our finding that the protective association was almost 
exclusively concentrated among users reporting ‘‘good’’ effec­
tiveness. At the same time, no significant benefit was observed 
for users reporting ‘‘poor’’ effectiveness. However, the situation 
in middle-income countries, such as China and Mexico in this 
study, is likely more complex, where individuals may face multi­
ple barriers in obtaining a hearing aid. Systemic obstacles 
include fragmented reimbursement systems often lacking public 
coverage for older adults40 and policy priorities that may favor 
high-cost interventions like pediatric cochlear implants over 
affordable hearing aids for the large elderly population.41,42 A se­
vere shortage of professional services compounds this. For 
instance, China has an extremely low ratio of audiologists to 
population (<2 per million), which limits their capacity for 
adequate consultation and fitting.43 This professional vacuum 
may partly explain the lower rates of reported device effective­
ness in this region (46.0% vs. 64.4% in high-income countries). 
Economically, even if the absolute price is lower, the relative 
cost of a hearing aid can be a substantial out-of-pocket expense, 
acting as a stringent filter.44 In light of these potential barriers, 
successfully acquiring a hearing aid may reflect not only an indi­
vidual’s health needs but also their economic capacity, higher 
health literacy, strong motivation, and rich social capital.37,38
Therefore, the extent to which the strong association between 
hearing aid use and lower dementia risk is influenced by this se­
lection effect, in addition to the intervention’s intrinsic benefits, 
requires careful consideration.
Consequently, attributing the observed effect entirely to 
the hearing aid while ignoring these powerful potential con­
founders is challenging. In summary, our research reveals that 
the association of hearing aid use must be interpreted within 
the socioeconomic and policy context in which it occurs. In mid­
dle-income countries, expanding basic access and affordability 
may be a primary policy challenge. In high-income countries, the 
policy focus might shift more toward optimizing intervention 
effectiveness. Future research must look beyond simple in­
come-level classifications to prospectively measure and analyze 
these key contextual moderators to more accurately assess the

potential role of hearing intervention in global efforts to reduce

Key strengths of our study include a large and diverse sample, 
harmonized multi-country data, consideration of hearing aid 
effectiveness, and extensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses. 
By pooling harmonized data from seven large, population-based 
cohort studies across 33 countries, we achieved an exceptional 
sample size and diversity, increasing both statistical power and 
generalizability. The consistent associations observed across 
varied settings suggest a robust and globally relevant link be­
tween hearing aid and probable dementia risk. Importantly, we 
went beyond the binary classification of hearing aid use by 
distinguishing between effective and ineffective use, which 
reduced exposure misclassification and revealed more nuanced 
associations. Extensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses 
further supported the robustness of our findings across different 
populations and assumptions. Finally, outcome misclassification 
was minimized through a probable dementia definition incorpo­
rating cognitive testing, functional assessment, and informant 
reports, rather than relying solely on self-report.
In conclusion, this large, multi-cohort observational analysis 
demonstrates a significant association between effective hear­
ing aid use and a lower risk of probable dementia. Given that 
hearing loss is one of the leading, potentially modifiable risk 
factors for dementia and that hearing healthcare is globally 
underutilized, this association is of significant public health inter­
est. The stronger association observed in middle-income coun­
tries, which have lower hearing aid adoption rates, and among 
unmarried individuals and those with lower education, suggests 
that targeting hearing interventions may be particularly impactful 
for those with limited resources and individuals at the higher risk. 
Therefore, greater investment in hearing healthcare and its inte­
gration into healthy aging strategies warrants further consider­
ation as part of global efforts to address dementia risk. However, 
future research is essential to confirm these associational find­
ings and investigate potential causality. Such studies should 
move beyond the limitations of self-report by utilizing objective 
measures like formal audiometry and real-world usage-tracking 
data, integrating country-level metrics to explain regional 
heterogeneity, and prioritizing the inclusion of underrepresented 
populations from low-resource settings to ensure findings are 
globally representative and can inform equitable public health 
strategies.
Limitations of the study
First, as an observational study, we cannot definitively prove a 
causal relationship between hearing aid use and dementia risk. 
We attempted to mitigate this by using IPTW to control for a 
wide range of known confounders, including most of the major 
risk factors identified by the Lancet Commission on dementia 
prevention.1 However, the potential for residual confounding 
by unmeasured factors can never be fully eliminated. To formally 
assess this possibility, we calculated the E-value to quantify the 
required strength of a potential unmeasured confounder to 
explain away our findings. Despite these methodological safe­
guards, the observed associations should be interpreted with 
appropriate caution. Second, self-reported measures for both 
hearing loss and hearing aid effectiveness may introduce bias. 
Some individuals with hearing loss may misjudge their own hear­
ing difficulties, leading them to be incorrectly categorized as hav­
ing normal hearing and thus excluded from the study. Since 
these individuals likely had milder hearing issues and a lower 
risk of dementia, their absence might make the dementia risk 
in those not using aids look higher than it actually is, leading to 
larger difference between hearing aid users and non-users. 
However, we found no significant difference in probable demen­
tia risk between participants reporting poor hearing aid effective­
ness and non-users. The fact that the risk for these two groups 
was nearly identical suggests that our findings remain robust 
despite the potential exclusion of these unrecognized cases. 
Furthermore, the self-reported data do not differentiate by 
severity, which could confound our findings on effectiveness, 
as individuals with milder impairment may be more likely to 
report a positive outcome. Self-reported effectiveness can also 
be confounded by factors like user expectations, where some 
participants classified as having ‘‘poor effectiveness’’ may 
have had adequate correction but higher expectations. Howev­
er, such self-reported measures are often necessary in large- 
scale cohort studies where objective testing is not feasible.45,46
Their use is also supported by prior research showing a strong 
correlation between self-reported and objective hearing loss,47
and our reliance on patient-reported outcomes aligns with stan­
dard practice for the inherently subjective construct of effective­
ness.46 Despite these potential inaccuracies, the clear contrast 
in probable dementia outcomes between the effectiveness 
groups suggests that our classification remains meaningful. 
Future studies may benefit from validated instruments, such as 
the Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit (APHAB) or the In­
ternational Outcome Inventory for Hearing Aids (IOI-HA), to more 
comprehensively evaluate perceived hearing aid benefit and 
user outcomes.48,49 Third, our criteria for probable dementia 
have several limitations. The cognitive assessments were not 
exhaustive. Additionally, the components of our composite 
definition varied across cohorts, including the inconsistent use 
of the informant-based IQCODE and self-report criteria. Howev­
er, we conducted sensitivity analyses that separately excluded 
cases identified by each source, and the consistency of our find­
ings confirmed their robustness against these definitional varia­
tions. Despite these harmonization challenges, an algorithmic 
approach is necessary, as relying solely on physician diagnoses 
can significantly under-detect dementia cases in population 
surveys.50 We also acknowledge that cognitive test results can 
fluctuate due to transient factors. Still, we sought to mitigate 
this by performing a sensitivity analysis using a stricter definition 
that excluded temporary cognitive impairment. Therefore, 
while imperfect, our validated algorithm, bolstered by these 
extensive sensitivity analyses, represents a robust approach 
for this research context. Fourth, despite including data from 
33 countries, our sample does not include any low-income na­
tions and lacks representation from key global regions, including 
most of Africa, South America, and Oceania. Therefore, the 
generalizability of our findings to these underrepresented popu­
lations is uncertain, and our results should be interpreted in this 
context. Further research across diverse global settings, partic­
ularly in low-resource settings, is essential to confirm these find­
ings and guide tailored interventions. Fifth, a further limitation is

(e.g., 3 years in MHAS vs. 2 years in most other cohorts) and spe­

who tried an aid but was not a regular user into the poor effec­

and underestimating the true association for the good effective­
ness group. We sought to mitigate this overall heterogeneity by 
employing a shared frailty model to account for cohort-level dif­
ferences and by conducting extensive sensitivity analyses to 
confirm the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, we 
acknowledge that these statistical adjustments cannot entirely 
eliminate all potential biases arising from such inconsistencies. 
Sixth, potential limitations related to the timing of assessments 
and diagnoses should be noted. Large-scale epidemiological 
studies rely on data collected at discrete time points (typically 
every 2 years in these cohorts), which can lead to interval 
censoring, where the exact timing of probable dementia onset 
between waves may be missed or inaccurately recorded.51,52
Although the relatively common 2-year interval may reduce this 
issue compared to longer intervals, some imprecision remains. 
Additionally, potential reverse causality exists, whereby early, 
undiagnosed cognitive decline might influence hearing aid use 
rather than vice versa. To mitigate this latter concern, we per­
formed a sensitivity analysis excluding participants diagnosed 
with dementia within the first three years of follow-up; the main 
findings remained robust. Finally, although our follow-up was 
longer than most studies, it may not fully capture the effects of 
mid-life hearing loss on dementia, as many participants were 
already older at baseline. Ideally, tracking should start in mid- 
life when hearing loss is most critical. Since our cohorts focused 
on individuals aged 55 years and above, we may have missed 
the benefits of earlier intervention. However, despite challenges 
such as dropout in long follow-ups, our multi-year tracking re­
mains a notable strength.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS
We conducted a longitudinal analysis using harmonized individual-level data from seven population-based aging cohort studies: the 
China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, China),53 the Korean Longitudinal Study of Aging (KLOSA, South Ko­
rea),54 the English Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSA, England, UK),55 the Survey of Health, Aging and Retirement in Europe 
(SHARE, Continental Europe and Israel),56 the Irish Longitudinal Study on Aging (TILDA, Ireland),57 the U.S. Health and Retirement 
Study (HRS, United States),58 and the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS, Mexico).59 These surveys collect comparable 
information on hearing, health, and cognition. The analytic dataset drew from CHARLS waves 1–5 (2011–2020), KLOSA waves 
1–7 (2006–2018), ELSA waves 2–10 (2004–2020), SHARE waves 1–9 (2004–2021; excluding wave 3, which lacked health data), 
TILDA waves 1–4 (2009–2016), HRS waves 4–15 (1998–2020), and MHAS waves 3–6 (2012–2021). Except for MHAS (3-year interval), 
all other cohorts had a 2-year follow-up interval.
The selection of cohorts for this analysis was guided by the need for longitudinally comparable data suitable for survival analysis. 
While the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) network is extensive, the following international sister studies were necessarily 
excluded due to specific methodological limitations that would have compromised the validity of the pooled time-to-event analysis. 
A detailed, cohort-by-cohort justification is provided below:
Six studies excluded due to insufficient longitudinal data. The Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI), the Brazilian Longitudinal 
Study of Aging (ELSI), and the Malaysia Aging and Retirement Survey (MARS) were excluded from our analysis, as only a single wave 
of data was available at the time of our analysis, precluding any longitudinal or survival analysis. The Northern Ireland Cohort for the 
Longitudinal Study of Aging (NICOLA) was excluded as only two waves of data were available, providing insufficient follow-up time for 
robust time-to-event modeling. The Japanese Study of Aging and Retirement (JSTAR) was excluded because cognitive assessments 
were not conducted in the third and fourth waves, which limited the available longitudinal data for our probable dementia outcome. 
The Costa Rican Longevity and Healthy Aging Study (CRELES) was excluded due to a significant and lengthy gap between follow-up 
waves, which could introduce substantial bias into time-to-event estimations.
Two studies excluded due to data accessibility or variable unavailability. The Health, Aging, and Retirement in Thailand (HART) was 
excluded because key cognitive and health variables required for our analysis were not publicly accessible. The Health and Aging in Africa 
was a Longitudinal Study of an INDEPTH Community in South Africa (HAALSI). While this cohort includes data on hearing aid use, it does 
not contain the specific variables needed to assess user-rated effectiveness, which was a primary exposure of interest in our study.
From each study, we included participants aged 55 and older who reported hearing loss at baseline and had data on their use of 
hearing aids. We excluded individuals with prevalent probable dementia at baseline or without follow-up data. Given that the prev­
alence of hearing aid use has remained relatively stable over time,60 and to maximize the sample size, the baseline was defined as the 
first wave in which they reported hearing loss and had data on hearing aid use. Participants were followed from baseline until the first 
occurrence of probable dementia, loss of follow-up, or the end of the study period, whichever came first. These seven surveys 
included in this study collectively cover 33 countries worldwide, providing a diverse and comprehensive dataset for analysis.
The final analytic sample consisted of 61,089 hearing-impaired participants (10,500 from CHARLS; 2,086 from KLOSA; 27,440

METHOD DETAILS
Assessment of hearing loss and hearing aid use
Hearing loss
Hearing loss was defined using two self-reported measures: 1) participants rated their hearing using a five-point scale (excellent, very 
good, good, fair, or poor); 2) hearing aid use status (Yes/No). Initial ascertainment of hearing aid use status preceded hearing loss 
evaluation: non-users provided unaided hearing ratings, while users reported function under optimally aided conditions. Participants 
who either rated their hearing as ‘‘fair’’ or ‘‘poor’’ or reported using a hearing aid were classified as having hearing loss at that wave.62
This combined definition captures individuals with self-perceived hearing difficulties as well as those who might have corrected their 
hearing loss with a device. Unlike the cognitive assessments where proxy informants were sometimes used, information regarding 
hearing status and hearing aid use relied solely on participant self-report.
Hearing aid use
Hearing aid use status was determined by the question, ‘‘Do you ever/usually/normally wear a hearing aid?’’ (Yes/No) asked at base­
line and follow-ups. In our analyses, those who answered ‘‘Yes’’ at baseline were considered hearing aid users, and those who 
answered ‘‘No’’ were considered non-users. For the primary analysis, hearing aid use status was determined at baseline; two sensi­
tivity analyses described in the statistical analysis section exclude (i) participants who initiated use during follow-up and (ii) baseline 
users who discontinued use during the first round of follow-up.
Hearing aid effectiveness
Hearing aid effectiveness was self-reported by the participants. After reporting hearing aid use, participants were asked to evaluate 
their hearing with the hearing aid, if applicable. Hearing aid users rated their aided hearing on the same five-point scale as above. We 
categorized hearing aid users into two effectiveness groups based on this self-evaluation: good effectiveness (if the person rated their 
hearing with the aid as ‘‘excellent’’, ‘‘very good’’, or ‘‘good’’) and poor effectiveness (if their aided hearing was ‘‘fair’’ or ‘‘poor’’). Thus, 
good effectiveness indicates that the hearing aid was perceived to substantially improve hearing, whereas poor effectiveness sug­
gests limited benefit from the device. Participants who did not use hearing aids at baseline constituted the reference (no aid) group for 
effectiveness analyses.
The specific definitions for hearing loss and hearing aid use for each cohort and wave are detailed in Table S2, while Table S3 out­
lines the harmonized strategy for measuring hearing aid use and effectiveness.
Identification of probable dementia
Considering the high prevalence of undiagnosed dementia cases,50 relying solely on physician-reported cases often leads to a 
significant underestimation of the true prevalence. To address this and capture cases that might otherwise be overlooked, an oper­
ational definition adapted in this study conforms to criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth 
edition, for estimation of probable dementia. Following these diagnostic criteria, participants were classified as having probable de­
mentia during follow-up if they met one of the following criteria: (1) concurrent cognitive and functional impairment identified in the 
same wave, or (2) a self-reported physician diagnosis of dementia at any wave. This approach is the standard method used across 
this family of cohort studies. Previous research has validated this algorithmic approach, demonstrating its high concordance with 
formal clinical diagnoses.63–67
Cognitive impairment
Cognitive function was assessed in three domains: memory, orientation, and executive function. Memory was evaluated using im­
mediate and delayed recall of word lists (10-word lists in all cohorts except an 8-word list in MHAS), with a total memory score ranging 
from 0 to 20 (0–16 in MHAS). Temporal orientation was measured by asking participants to identify the current date (day, month, year) 
and day of the week (score 0–4), except for MHAS where only the current date (day, month, year) was used (score 0–3). Executive 
function was measured with either a serial 7s subtraction test (subtracting seven from 100 repeatedly; used in HRS, CHARLS, 
KLOSA) or a verbal fluency test (naming as many animals as possible in 60 s; used in SHARE, ELSA, TILDA, MHAS). While the tasks 
differ, both are established measures of executive function with high reliability and validity, and they have demonstrated strong 
concordance in identifying impairment, which supports their comparability in this harmonized analysis. In line with the established 
and harmonized practice across these seven cohort studies, impairment on each cognitive test was defined as a score at least 
1.5 standard deviations below the mean for the participant’s age (within the 50–80 years range) and education level.63,65–70
Additionally, in three studies (HRS, MHAS, ELSA), a proxy-informant questionnaire was used when participants were unable to be 
directly interviewed. Specifically, the short form of the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE)71 was 
administered to a knowledgeable informant, who was asked about changes in the participant’s memory and daily functioning over 
the past two years. An average IQCODE score, ranging from 3.3 to 3.6 (on a 1–5 scale) was taken as evidence of significant cognitive 
decline.71 We considered a participant to have cognitive impairment if they had impairments in at least two of the cognitive tests

Functional impairment
Functional status was universally assessed across all seven cohorts via self-reported difficulty with basic activities of daily living

bladder/bowel). Participants who reported difficulty with one or more ADLs were classified as having functional impairment.
Self-reported dementia diagnosis
In five cohorts (CHARLS, SHARE, ELSA, TILDA, HRS), participants (or proxies) were directly asked if a doctor had ever diagnosed 
them with dementia or Alzheimer’s disease (or ‘‘memory-related disease’’ in CHARLS and wave4-9 in HRS). An affirmative response 
was treated as a dementia case.
Definition of probable dementia
Probable dementia ascertainment employed a harmonized protocol. Cognitive impairment was defined as performance >1.5 SD 
below mean for the participant’s age (within 50–80 years) and education level in ≥2 of three domains (memory, temporal orientation, 
executive function) and/or an IQCODE score ≥3.6. The IQCODE instrument, however, was administered only in the HRS, ELSA, and 
MHAS cohorts, where cognitive impairment was incorporated using either criterion. In other cohorts, cognitive impairment relied 
exclusively on domain deficits. Functional impairment was universally determined through ADLs limitations, with one or more ADL 
impairments constituting dysfunction. Probable dementia classification required either: 1) concurrent cognitive and functional 
impairment, or 2) physician-diagnosed dementia. In CHARLS and HRS waves 4–7 – where physician diagnosis specifically refer­
enced memory-related diseases rather than dementia – self-reported memory-related disease diagnoses served as diagnostic prox­
ies. For cohorts lacking physician diagnosis data (KLOSA, SHARE, MHAS), probable dementia classification depended exclusively 
on cognitive-functional impairment. Wave-specific binary probable dementia indicators were subsequently generated, from which 
time-to-dementia variables were derived with follow-up duration calculated from baseline until the first occurrence of probable de­
mentia, loss of follow-up, or the end of the study period, whichever came first. These standardized variables were constructed for 
cross-cohort pooled analyses. Detailed information on each cohort’s specific instruments and diagnostic criteria is provided in 
Table S2, while Table S3 presents the harmonized strategy for the outcome variables.
Covariates
Based on established dementia risk factors, we included the following baseline individual-level covariates in all analyses12,14,72: age 
(years), gender (women/men), education level (categorized as primary school or less, secondary school, college or above), marital 
status (married/partnered, unmarried/divorced/widowed), family income level (categorized as low, middle and high level) current 
smoking status (yes/no), current alcohol use (yes/no), physical activity (engages in any moderate or vigorous activity/none), diag­
nosed hypertension (yes/no), diagnosed diabetes (yes/no), diagnosed cardiovascular disease (yes/no), health insurance status 
(yes/no), intergenerational support (yes/no), cognitive impairment domains (including memory impairment, temporal orientation 
impairment, executive function impairment; all yes/no), as well as country-level covariates: Universal health coverage (UHC) and so­
cio-demographic index (SDI). These variables were harmonized across cohorts as closely as possible (details in Table S4).
QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Missing values for outcome measures and covariates varied by cohort (for example, up to 29.8% for cognitive tests in CHARLS and 
up to 24.2% for ADL measures in TILDA). The detailed information on missing data for each wave within each cohort is provided in 
Tables S5, S6, S7, S8, S9, S10, and S11. Most missingness was deemed to be Missing at Random (MAR), based on the assump­
tion that the missingness could be explained by other observed covariates. Therefore, we performed 25 imputations via chained 
equations (using the mice package in R) to fill in missing outcome variables and covariates for each wave within each cohort,73
thereby reducing potential bias from attrition or non-response. The imputation model included wave-specific covariates—age 
(years), gender (women/men), education (primary school or less; secondary school; college or above), marital status (married/part­

To account for potential confounding in the non-randomized comparison of non-users vs. hearing aid users (and poor hearing aid 
effective vs. good hearing aid effective users), we employed an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach. We esti­
mated propensity scores using logistic regression to determine the likelihood of hearing aid use (yes vs. no) at baseline, including all 
covariates listed above. Similarly, for analyses involving hearing aid effectiveness, we used multinomial logistic regression to esti­
mate propensity scores for three groups (no aid, good effectiveness, poor effectiveness), given the covariates. Each participant 
was then weighted by the inverse of the probability of their observed treatment status. We examined covariate balance before 
and after weighting by calculating standardized mean differences (SMD) between groups, and the groups were considered homo­
geneous for each variable when the SMD was ≤0.1 after weighting.74
For probable dementia incidence, we fitted Cox proportional hazards models (with time since baseline as the timescale) to estimate 
hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the association between hearing aid use and probable dementia. To 
address between-cohort heterogeneity, we included cohort as a shared frailty in the model. The proportional hazards (PH) assump­
tion was tested graphically using plots of the log-cumulative hazard. This assumption was verified not only for the primary harmo­
nized cohort but also within each individual cohort and for the analyses stratified by country income level, as the curves for the 
compared groups were consistently found to be approximately parallel. The primary Cox model compared hearing aid users to 
non-users among hearing-impaired individuals in the harmonized cohort and then performed stratified analyses by country income 
level (High-income vs. Middle-income) to evaluate potential variations in the associations. As a supplementary analysis, we also fitted 
the model within each individual cohort to examine cohort-specific associations. All models were weighted by IPTWs.
We conducted a parallel set of IPTW-weighted Cox models to examine the effectiveness of hearing aids, treating non-users as the 
reference group. In these models, we estimated the hazard of probable dementia for two groups of hearing aid users: those with good 
and poor effectiveness, relative to hearing-impaired non-users. Separate models for these effectiveness analyses were also con­
ducted within each country’s income level and each individual cohort.
To explore potential association modification, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by the following key sociodemographic 
factors: age group (55–69 vs. ≥70 years), gender, education level (primary or less vs. secondary and above), marital status (married/ 
partnered vs. unmarried/others), family income level (low, middle, vs. high), health insurance status (yes vs. no), and SDI (low, middle, 
vs. high). Within each stratum, we fitted the IPTW-weighted Cox model for both hearing aid use and effectiveness. To ensure statis­
tical stability, analyses were restricted to subgroups with at least 100 participants.75 p-values for the subgroup estimates were 
adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. We formally tested for interaction by adding product terms to the 
Cox models and assessing their significance with likelihood ratio tests.
We carried out several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, to reduce potential reverse causation, we 
excluded individuals who developed probable dementia within the first 3 years of follow-up and refit the models. Second, since de­
mentia risk accelerates at older ages, we restricted the sample to participants aged 60 and above at baseline to see if results were 
consistent in this older subset. Third, we limited the analysis to participants who had at least two follow-up waves after baseline, 
ensuring they were observed over time beyond a single assessment. Fourth, we applied a more stringent definition of probable de­
mentia by requiring the cognitive impairment to be sustained. If a participant met cognitive impairment criteria at one wave but then 
improved by ≥1 SD in a subsequent wave (suggesting transient delirium or measurement error), we did not count the initial event as 
probable dementia. Fifth, to test the robustness of our findings to the heterogeneous components of our probable dementia defini­
tion, we conducted two separate sensitivity analyses. The first excluded cases were identified via the informant-based IQCODE 
(which was not available in all cohorts), while the second excluded cases were based on the broad self-reported criterion of mem­
ory-related disease. Sixth, to address exposure misclassification resulting from changes in hearing aid use over time, we conducted 
two sensitivity analyses: one excluding participants who initiated hearing aid use during follow-up, and another excluding those who 
used hearing aids at baseline but subsequently discontinued them in the first round of follow-up (non-persistent use). The ELSA and 
TILDA cohorts were excluded from both analyses due to the timing of their hearing aid measurements. Seventh, to enhance mea­
surement validity, we repeated the analyses while restricting multiple imputations exclusively to missing covariates and deliberately 
omitting imputations for the outcome variable. Eighth, to minimize potential bias introduced by imputation, we conducted additional 
analyses using unimputed data. Ninth, we used competing risk models with death as a competing risk of probable dementia. TILDA 
was not included in this sensitivity analysis since death was not measured in TILDA. Tenth, to account for country-level context, our 
primary models included the SDI as a covariate. To assess the robustness of our findings to the choice of this indicator and better 
understand the role of national-level factors, we also conducted sensitivity analyses using separate models that controlled for the 
UHC index instead of the SDI. To formally assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors on our primary associa­
tions, we calculated the E-value. The E-value quantifies the minimum strength of association that a potential unmeasured confounder 
would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to completely negate the observed association.76 All analyses were 
conducted using R version 4.3.2, with significance levels set at p < 0.05.
