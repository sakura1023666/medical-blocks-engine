Abstract
Background  The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), an established measure of peripheral insulin sensitivity, 
contributes to stratifying the risk of cardio-cerebrovascular events. Nevertheless, the association between long-
term eGDR exposure and stroke incidence throughout all stages (0–4) of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) 
syndrome remains unknown.
Methods  A cohort of 5248 individuals was drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study 
(CHARLS). For each participant, eGDR values for the years 2012 and 2015 were calculated using the equation: 
21.158 − [0.090 × WC (cm)] − [3.407 × HTN (presence = 1)] − [0.551 × HbA1c (%)]. Cumulative eGDR was calculated as 
(eGDR2012 + eGDR2015)/2* time (2015–2012). K-means clustering was used to analyse eGDR values from both 2012 
and 2015 to identify distinct change patterns. To assess associations with stroke risk, we utilised multivariable logistic 
regression and restricted cubic spline models.
Results  During the 2015–2018 follow-up period, a total of 336 incident stroke cases were documented. Four distinct 
eGDR change patterns were identified. In fully adjusted models, compared with the participants in the persistent 
low pattern (Class 2), those in the moderate–high stable (OR 0.43, 95% CI: 0.31–0.58), stable high (OR 0.29, 0.19–0.43), 
and rapid decrease (OR 0.66, 0.47–0.91) patterns exhibited significantly lower stroke risk. Furthermore, each 1-unit 
increase in cumulative eGDR was associated with a 5% reduction in stroke odds (OR 0.95, 0.93–0.96). Restricted cubic 
spline analysis confirmed a linear inverse relationship between cumulative eGDR and stroke risk (P < 0.001; P for 
nonlinearity = 0.259).

​Introduction
Stroke poses a critical public health threat in China and 
is characterised by substantial and growing burdens 
of morbidity and disability [1, 2]. Cardiovascular–kid­
ney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome—a pathophysiologi­
cal nexus interconnecting obesity, diabetes, and chronic 
kidney disease—increasingly frames our understanding 
of this challenge by amplifying vulnerability to cardio-
cerebrovascular events [3]. Susceptibility linked to CKM 
syndrome is not static but follows a graded progression; 
individuals in stages 3–4 face a disproportionately greater 
stroke risk than those in stages 0–2 [4]. This graded risk 
pattern underscores the necessity of identifying modifi­
able risk factors, which can inform early intervention 
strategies for stroke prevention in the CKM population.
The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), a 
proxy measure of peripheral insulin sensitivity, is 
computed 
as 
follows: 
eGDR = 21.158 − (0.090 × WC 
[cm]) − (3.407 × HTN 
[presence = 1]) − (0.551 × HbA1c 
[%]), with higher values signifying increased insulin sen­
sitivity [5]. Low eGDR not only corresponds to severe 
insulin resistance but is also related to stroke occur­
rence through multiple biological mechanisms [6, 7]. At 
the vascular level, insulin resistance compromises endo­
thelial function by reducing nitric oxide synthesis and 
increasing endothelin-1 production, leading to impaired 
vasodilation, increased vasoconstriction, and altered vas­
cular reactivity. This endothelial dysfunction promotes 
atherosclerotic plaque formation and destabilisation, 
which are critical events in stroke pathogenesis [8–10]. 
Systemically, it perpetuates a chronic inflammatory state 
characterised by increased cytokine levels—including 
high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleu­
kin-6 (IL-6), and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)—
which together impair endothelial integrity, accelerate 
atherogenesis, and foster a prothrombotic milieu [10–
12]. Furthermore, insulin resistance exacerbates oxidative 
stress through dual pathways: increasing reactive oxygen 
species (ROS) generation and decreasing antioxidant 
defences, culminating in vascular cellular damage and 
dysfunction [13]. These pathways are further amplified by 
associated metabolic disturbances—including dyslipidae­
mia, sympathetic nervous system activation, and hyper­
coagulability mediated by elevated plasminogen activator 
inhibitor-1—collectively increasing susceptibility to cere­
brovascular events [14–16].
Substantial evidence indicates that reduced eGDR con­
tributes to cerebrocardiovascular pathogenesis. Dong et 
al. [17] reported a 42% increase in cardiovascular dis­
ease risk (HR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.36–1.48) per 1-unit eGDR 
decrease in patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. 
Similarly, Zabala et al. [18] reported a 78% higher stroke

Liang et al. [19] revealed a U-shaped relationship: cardio­

adverse cardio-cerebrovascular events (RR = 1.84; 95% CI:

However, given that eGDR is a dynamic parameter, a 
single assessment may not fully capture its cumulative 
burden. Although cumulative exposure to cardiovascu­
lar risk factors—not baseline levels—is strongly associ­
ated with long-term clinical outcomes, the influence of 
cumulative eGDR on stroke in the context of CKM syn­
drome has not been specifically assessed [4, 6]. This study 
examined how longitudinal eGDR variations are related 
to stroke incidence across all stages of CKM syndrome 
(0–4) in the CHARLS cohort, addressing a critical gap in 
evidence.
Methods
Study population
CHARLS (China Health and Retirement Longitudinal 
Study), a national population-based cohort, enrolled 
Chinese participants aged ≥ 45 years. This study was car­
ried out jointly by Peking University’s National School 
of Development and the Chinese Academy of Social 
Sciences. It utilised a multistage, stratified sampling 
approach, with four waves of data collection conducted 
in 2012 (baseline), 2013, 2015, and 2018. All surveys fol­
lowed standardised protocols to maintain data quality 
and comparability. Further methodological details are 
available in prior publications [21, 22].
Participants were included if they met the following 
criteria: (1) complete data on CKM syndrome staging 
(stages 0–4); (2) no stroke history and available stroke 
status data from waves 1 to 3 (2012–2015); (3) acces­
sible stroke outcome data at wave 4 (2018); and (4) com­
plete data for estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) 
calculation (without outliers) at waves 1 or 3. Individu­
als not meeting all these criteria were excluded from the 
analysis.
Ethical approval for the CHARLS study was obtained 
from the IRB of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). 
All participants provided written informed consent prior 
to enrolment.
Assessment of eGDR, cumulative eGDR, and eGDR change 
patterns
Waist circumference (WC) was measured at the umbili­
cal level in standing participants during normal tidal 
breathing [23]. Hypertension (HTN) was defined by: (1) 
prior physician diagnosis, (2) current antihypertensive 
treatment, or (3) blood pressure ≥ 140/90  mmHg. Gly­
cated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measurements from whole 
blood samples underwent NHANES-standardised assay 
calibration [24]. eGDR was derived using the validated 
equation: 
21.158 − [0.090 × WC 
(cm)] − [3.407 × HTN 
(presence = 1)] − [0.551 × HbA1c (%)]. Here, WC (cm), 
HTN (1 = present; 0 = absent), and HbA1c (%) operation­
alised these variables [5, 25]. Cumulative eGDR exposure 
was computed as follows: (eGDR₂₀₁₂ + eGDR₂₀₁₅)/2 × (tim
e₂₀₁₅—time₂₀₁₂) [26]. Participants were subsequently clas­
sified into four distinct eGDR change patterns through 
k-means clustering analysis using eGDR values from both 
2012 and 2015 as input features: Class 1 (moderate–high 
stable), Class 2 (persistent low), Class 3 (stable high), and 
Class 4 (rapid decrease).
CKM syndrome staging classification (0–4)
CKM syndrome staging at baseline (2012, Wave 1) was 
determined following the American Heart Association 
framework, which categorises the condition into five pro­
gressive stages (0–4) [3]. Stage 0 characterises individuals 
without metabolic or cardiovascular risk factors; Stage 1 
manifests overweight/obesity or prediabetes;
Stage 2 is defined by established conditions, including 
type 2 diabetes (T2DM), hypertension (HTN), hypertri­
glyceridaemia, and chronic kidney disease (CKD); Stage 
3 involves subclinical cardiovascular impairment, such 
as asymptomatic atherosclerosis or left ventricular dys­
function; and Stage 4 represents clinical cardiovascular 
disease—such as coronary artery disease (CAD), heart 
failure (HF), stroke, peripheral artery disease (PAD), and 
atrial fibrillation (AF)—in patients with established CKM 
pathophysiology.
Outcome ascertainment
The incidence of stroke during follow-up served as the 
primary outcome. In Wave 4, stroke events were ascer­
tained when participants reported affirmative responses 
to the physician-diagnosed question: “Have you been 
diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?” This case identifica­
tion approach has been validated in prior investigations 
[27, 28].

At baseline (Wave 1, 2011–2012), trained personnel 
administered standardised questionnaires to obtain 
sociodemographic and health-related information. Col­
lected variables included age, sex, marital status, and

Results
Baseline characteristics by eGDR change classes

tion. The CHARLS baseline cohort (2011–2012) initially 
comprised 17,708 eligible participants. Participants with 
incomplete CKM syndrome staging data (n = 8307) were 
excluded, leaving 9401 individuals with documented 
CKM stages 0–4. Among these, sequential exclusions 
were applied for the following: history of stroke or miss­
ing stroke status during the baseline and follow-up 
period from waves 1 through 3 (n = 1828); absence of 
stroke outcome data at wave 4 (n = 710); and incomplete 
eGDR measurements or outlier values at baseline or wave

the final analytical cohort comprised 5248 participants 
categorised into four classes on the basis of eGDR change 
patterns (Class 1, n = 1883; Class 2, n = 1121; Class 3, 
n = 1410; and Class 4, n = 834).

Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD when 
normally distributed or as median (IQR) otherwise. Cat­
egorical data are reported as counts and percentages. 
Baseline comparisons were performed using χ2/Fisher 
exact tests (expected frequencies < 5) for categorical vari­
ables, t-test for normally distributed continuous vari­
ables, and Mann‒Whitney U tests for skewed continuous 
variables.
To characterise longitudinal eGDR patterns from 2012 
to 2015, we applied the k-means clustering algorithm 
using eGDR measurements from both time points (2012 
and 2015) as input variables for each participant. This 
bivariate approach enabled the algorithm to capture het­
erogeneity in both baseline insulin sensitivity status and 
temporal evolution. The k-means method, an unsuper­
vised machine learning technique, groups observations 
by minimising within-cluster variance. The optimal num­
ber of clusters was determined using the elbow method, 
which evaluates the reduction in within-cluster sum 
of squares as the number of clusters increases. A clear 
inflection point emerged at K = 4, which suggests that 
four clusters provided an optimal balance between model 
fit and complexity (Fig. 2A). On the basis of this solution, 
the participants were categorised into four distinct tra­
jectory groups on the basis of their proximity to the final 
cluster centroids (Fig. 2B).
Cumulative eGDR exposure was analysed as both a 
continuous measure and in tertiles to evaluate its dose‒
response relationship with stroke incidence. Given the 
low incidence of stroke (6.40%) in our cohort, multivari­
able logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios 
(ORs), which approximate hazard ratios (HRs) under 
the rare disease assumption [29]. To address potential 
limitations from follow-up duration, we conducted Cox 
proportional hazards models as sensitivity analyses.

fidence intervals (CIs) across sequential models: Model 
1 (unadjusted crude analysis); Model 2 (adjusted for

Discussion
This study investigated the associations between stroke 
risk and eGDR change patterns and cumulative eGDR 
across cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syn­
drome stages 0–4. Using k-means clustering, we iden­
tified four distinct eGDR patterns over the 3-year 
observation period. Compared with the reference group 
with persistently low eGDR levels (Class 2), which exhib­
ited the highest stroke incidence (12.85%), all other 
groups showed substantially reduced stroke risk after 
multivariable adjustment. Additionally, cumulative eGDR 
was independently associated with stroke occurrence, 
with a dose-dependent relationship observed across ter­
tiles. Participants with higher cumulative eGDR values 
experienced progressively lower stroke risk. These find­
ings suggest that long-term maintenance of elevated 
eGDR levels is associated with reduced stroke risk in this 
population.
The current results align with those of previous pro­
spective cohort investigations demonstrating an inverse 
relationship between the eGDR and incident cardio-cere­
brovascular outcomes [31, 32]. For instance, an analysis 
of the CHARLS by Jiang et al. revealed that a per-SD 
eGDR increase resulted in a 25.4% lower stroke risk in 
dysglycaemic adults ≥ 45 years [33]. Similarly, Tao et al. 
[34] reported a 39.5% reduction in stroke incidence per 
1-unit eGDR increase in a general Chinese population. 
This pattern is further supported by Zhang et al.’s anal­
ysis of the UK Biobank cohort, which revealed progres­
sively lower cardiovascular disease occurrence across 
ascending eGDR quartiles among participants in CKM 
syndrome stages 0–3 [35]. The consistency of this rela­
tionship across populations with varying metabolic 
characteristics strengthens the evidence for an intercon­
nection between insulin sensitivity and cerebrovascular 
health.
Our investigation expands upon existing research 
through its specific focus on CKM syndrome stages 
0–4 and longitudinal eGDR assessment. While previous 
reports primarily utilised single eGDR measurements 
[33, 36], our analysis of eGDR patterns over time pro­
vides a new understanding of the dynamic interconnec­
tion between insulin sensitivity and stroke occurrence 
in this population with multisystem involvement. This 
methodological approach, involving k-means clustering 
of serial eGDR measurements, builds upon the analytical 
framework demonstrated in prior longitudinal studies of 
metabolic indices [4]. Furthermore, our inclusion of indi­
viduals across the complete CKM spectrum, including 
stage 4, represents an important extension beyond earlier 
work limited to initial disease phases [37], enabling a 
more thorough characterisation of the eGDR-stroke 
interconnection throughout CKM progression.
This inverse eGDR-stroke risk association can be 
attributed to multiple interconnected biological path­
ways, reflecting the complex relationship between insu­
lin resistance (IR) and cerebrovascular pathophysiology. 
IR contributes to endothelial dysfunction by impairing 
nitric oxide (NO) synthesis while promoting endothe­
lin-1 release, which collectively disrupts vascular homeo­
stasis and facilitates atherosclerotic plaque instability [38, 
39]. The link between eGDR and stroke further under­
scores the importance of NO in preserving endothe­
lial integrity and modulating inflammatory responses, 
as demonstrated in studies by Huang et al. [40]. Their 
work confirmed that reduced eGDR is associated with 
increased 
inflammatory 
markers, 
highlighting 
the 
involvement of immune pathways in stroke pathogenesis 
[41]. Key proinflammatory mediators—hs-CRP, IL-6, and 
TNF-α—increase vascular risk by exacerbating endothe­
lial injury, promoting thrombus formation, and accelerat­
ing atherosclerosis [20].
This study has several methodological strengths that 
distinguish it from prior investigations. First, unlike pre­
vious research reliant on single measurements, we iden­
tified clinically meaningful subphenotypes by applying 
k-means clustering to baseline eGDR, revealing hetero­
geneous risk profiles that were not apparent when the 
population was treated as homogeneous. Second, the 
combination of this baseline stratification with cumula­
tive eGDR exposure represents a more nuanced approach 
to assessing insulin resistance burden, capturing both 
cross-sectional heterogeneity and temporal exposure. 
Third, in contrast to prior studies that were largely con­
fined to diabetic cohorts or excluded patients with car­
diovascular disease (CKM stage 4), our investigation 
captured the complete CKM continuum (stages 0–4), 
thereby strengthening the applicability of the results to 
unselected clinical populations. Fourth, we minimised 
residual confounding through sequential adjustments 
for sociodemographic, lifestyle, anthropometric, and 
cardiometabolic factors in multivariable models. Finally, 
sensitivity analyses validated the stability of the observed 
associations, reinforcing the robustness of our findings. 
These results support the hypothesis that eGDR monitor­
ing may help identify individuals at high risk of stroke.
Several 
limitations 
warrant 
consideration. 
First, 
although the low event rate supports the use of logis­
tic regression, our primary analysis did not account for 
the exact timing of events or potential censoring. How­
ever, since we conducted only one follow-up assessment 
from 2015 to 2018, there are no issues related to censor­
ing in our analysis. Future studies with extended follow-
up should employ time-to-event models to fully capture

Page 10 of 11

temporal dynamics [42]. Second, the final analytical 
cohort exclusively comprised adults aged above 45 years, 
which may limit generalisability to younger or ethnically 
diverse populations. In addition, the observational nature 
of this study fundamentally precludes causal inference. 
Despite extensive multivariable adjustment, residual con­
founding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. 
Moreover, reverse causality remains a possibility: sub­
clinical disease may lead to changes in body weight or 
physical activity, which in turn influence eGDRvalues. 
These bidirectional relationships cannot be definitively 
disentangled in our observational framework. Fourth, 
stroke outcomes were ascertained through self-reports 
rather than standardised clinical adjudication, introduc­
ing the possibility of diagnostic misclassification. Finally, 
the 6-year observation window might not be adequate for 
evaluating the long-term cerebrovascular consequences 
associated with insulin resistance. Future research 
involving extended follow-up durations, objectively veri­
fied endpoints, and more heterogeneous populations is 
warranted to validate our observations.
Conclusion
In individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–4, a signifi­
cant inverse association was observed between cumula­
tive eGDR and stroke risk. These findings suggest that 
identifying distinct eGDR trajectory patterns may help 
stratify stroke risk in this population.
