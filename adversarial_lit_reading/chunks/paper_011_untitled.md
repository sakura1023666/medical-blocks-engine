A B S T R A C T
Background: The relationship between cardiometabolic index (CMI) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains
unknown in the general population. Existing research has primarily concentrated on baseline CMI, not taking
into consideration the changes in CMI over time.
Objective: This study aimed to investigate the associations of baseline and changes in CMI with the risk of incident
CVD.
Methods: This study used data from two prospective cohorts: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
(CHARLS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). Changes in CMI were determined by total CMI,
calculated by CMI at baseline plus CMI at the second survey.
Results: A total of 8059 participants from CHARLS and 4279 from ELSA were included in baseline CMI analyses.
Participants with upper tertile of baseline CMI had increased risks of incident CVD than those with lower tertile
(CHARLS, HR 1.36, 95 % CI 1.21–1.53; ELSA, HR 1.24, 95 % CI 1.07–1.43). A total of 4946 participants from
CHARLS and 2371 from ELSA were included in CMI change analyses. For the changes in CMI analyses, partic-
ipants with tertile 3 showed an elevated risk of incident CVD than tertile 1 (CHARLS: HR 1.53, 95 % CI
1.29–1.81; ELSA: HR 1.41, 95 % CI 1.15–1.73). The restricted cubic spine models showed a nonlinear rela-
tionship between baseline and total CMI and incident CVD risks in the CHARLS, while a linear relationship in the
ELSA.
Conclusions: Both baseline and total CMI were associated with an increased risk of CVD. Long-term monitoring of
CMI levels is of great importance for CVD prevention in clinical practice.
1. Introduction
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of global mortality
and a major contributor to global health loss [1,2] The overall
prevalence of CVD nearly doubled from 271 million cases in 1990 to 523
million cases in 2019, while the number of deaths due to CVD increased
from 12.1 million to 18.6 million globally during the same period [3]
The projected increase in CVD prevalence, particularly among mid-
dle-aged and older adults, will place a substantial financial burden on
the healthcare system and pose urgent challenges to public health [4]

The association between insulin resistance, obesity, and CVD has
been well documented [9,10] The triglyceride/high-density lipoprotein
cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio, a good surrogate for insulin resistance,
serves as a robust predictor for CVD risk [11,12]. Waist-to-height ratio

calculated as TG/HDL-C ratio multiplied by WHtR, combining insulin
resistance and obesity parameters for the effective identification of
diabetes. The CMI has been reported to be a more effective predictor of
metabolic disorders and provide a comprehensive assessment of the
metabolic health, especially regarding the impact on abdominal fat
distribution and the protective effect of cholesterol, thus outperforming
traditional obesity and lipid indicators [15–18] Previous studies have
found that a higher CMI level is associated with an increased risk of
hypertension, kidney disease, and mortality [19–21] In cohort study of
3752 US adults aged ≥40 years, elevated CMI was significantly associ-
ated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality during an average
follow-up of 10.7 years. In addition, a recent study showed a significant
association between CMI and the risk of incident CVD in patients with
hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea [22] However, the relation-
ship between CMI and CVD remains unknown in the general population.
In addition, prior studies have largely focused on the impact of baseline
CMI on study outcomes, not taking into consideration the changes in
CMI over time. Recent studies have demonstrated that changes in CMI
component, including metabolic parameters and WC, are significantly
associated with incident CVD risks [23–26] Therefore, it is imperative to
investigate the associations of changes in CMI with the risk of incident
CVD.
In the current study, we used data from two prospective cohorts: the
China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the
English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). We aimed to investigate
the associations of baseline and changes in CMI with the risk of incident
CVD.
2. Methods
2.1. Study design and data sources
The CHARLS and ELSA are two ongoing prospective and nationally
representative cohorts of community-dwelling adults (aged ≥45 years in
the CHARLS and ≥50 years in the ELSA), which were conducted in
China and the United Kingdom, respectively. Further details regarding
the objectives, design, and methods of these two cohorts are available
elsewhere [27,28] The CHARLS received approval from the institutional
review board of Peking University, and the ELSA received approval from
the Ethics Review Committees of London Multi-Centre Research Ethics.
Informed consent was obtained from each participant in these two co-
horts and this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of
Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline
[29]
In this study, wave 1 (2011) of the CHARLS and wave 2 (2004–2005)
of the ELSA were served as the baseline. Wave 3 (2015) of the CHARLS
and wave 4 (2008–2009) of the ELSA were served as the second survey.
We used data from the baseline and the second survey to evaluate the
dynamic change of CMI. Subsequent waves of follow-up were used to
track outcomes until the final follow-up wave, which was wave 5 (2020)
of the CHARLS and wave 10 (2021–2022) of the ELSA.
The selection process of this study population is shown in Fig. 1.
Among 26,473 participants from the CHARLS and ELSA, we excluded
11,415 participants with missing data for calculating the CMI at base-
line. Moreover, 2720 participants with CVD at baseline or lost to follow-
up were also excluded. Finally, 12,338 eligible participants were
included in baseline CMI analyses. For the changes in CMI analyses, we

2.2. Assessment of cardiometabolic index
The CMI is calculated using the following formula: [14] CMI = TG
(mmol/L) / HDL-C (mmol/L) × WC (cm) / height (cm). CMI was treated
as a continuous variable and all enrolled participants were stratified into
tertiles based on CMI values. In addition, we employed the total CMI to
assess the changes in CMI. Total CMI was calculated by the CMI at
baseline plus the CMI at the second survey to account for cumulative
exposure to cardiometabolic risk. This approach aligns with prior
studies demonstrating that longitudinal changes in metabolic and other
indices improve CVD prediction [30–33] By integrating both timepoints,
total CMI captures sustained risk beyond a single measurement, miti-
gating regression dilution bias and reflecting the chronicity of metabolic
dysfunction.
2.3. Covariates
The following covariates were included: age, sex, education, marital
status, labor force status, smoking status, drinking status, history of
hypertension, diabetes, arthritis, chronic lung disease, and cancer, sys-
tolic blood pressure (SBP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), low-density
lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and C-reactive protein (CRP). Educa-
tion was classified into three levels: below high school, high school, and
college or above. Marital status was divided into two categories: married
or partnered and other marital status (separated, divorced, unmarried,
or widowed). Labor force status was divided into two categories:
working and not working. Smoking status was categorized as current
smokers and former/never smokers. Similarly, drinking status was
categorized as current drinkers and former/never drinkers.
2.4. Definitions of outcomes
The primary outcome of this study was incident CVD. Consistent
with
previous
studies,
[25,30]
CVD
was
ascertained
based
on
self-reported physician-diagnosed heart disease or stroke. Participants
were asked in each wave, "Have you been told by a doctor that you have
been diagnosed with a heart disease, including angina, heart attack,
congestive heart failure, and other heart problems?" and "Have you been
told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?" Those who
reported being diagnosed with heart disease or stroke were considered
to have CVD. In subsequent waves, participants were required to
confirm the existence of heart disease and stroke if they reported those
in the last wave. If participants disputed self-reported heart disease or
stroke from previous waves, they were corrected retrospectively.
In the baseline CMI analyses, follow-up was initiated from wave 1
(2012) of the CHARLS and wave 2 (2004–2005) of the ELSA. In the
changes in CMI analyses, follow-up was initiated from wave 3 (2015) of
the CHARLS and wave 4 (2008–2009) of the ELSA. The endpoint of
follow-up was at the first occurrence of CVD, death, or the censoring
date, whichever came first. The censoring date was defined as the date of
the last survey attended by each participant. Ideally, the last survey was
wave 5 (2020) of the CHARLS and wave 10 (2020–2021) of the ELSA.
Death data were available up to the last survey in the CHARLS, while
those data were only available up to wave 6 (2012–2013) in the ELSA
[34].
2.5. Statistical analyses
The idea of this study was conceived in April 2024, and the final data
acquisition and analysis were conducted in December 2024. For
descriptive statistics, continuous variables were expressed as mean and
standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), while
categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). The
missing rates of covariates were detailed in Supplemental Tables 1 and
2. Missing data of covariates were imputed using multiple imputations
with chained equations [35]

CVD, we utilized the Cox proportional hazards regression to calculate
the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95 % confidence interval (CI). Baseline CMI
was classified into three groups. T1 was the lower tertile, T2 was the
middle tertile, and T3 was the upper tertile. In the CHARLS, T1 of

repeated using the competing risk model; [36] (4) we repeated the main
analyses after further adjusting for the use of antihypertensive drugs and
antidiabetic drugs. In addition, the main analyses were repeated when
using the outcomes using the outcomes of heart disease and stroke
separately. Finally, we conducted stratified analyses by age (mid-

3. Results
3.1. Baseline characteristics of the study population
Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 8059 participants from
the CHARLS (mean age: 59.2 years, male: 47.4 %) and 4279 from the
ELSA (mean age: 64.7 years, male: 44.2 %) were included in the baseline
CMI analyses. Baseline characteristics of these participants with
different CMI tertiles are shown in Table 1. Participants with higher
tertiles of CMI were more likely to be working and tended to have a
history of hypertension and diabetes than those with the lowest tertile of
CMI. Participants with higher tertiles of CMI also exhibited higher levels
of SBP, HbA1c, and CRP in comparison to those with the lowest tertile of
CMI. In the analyses of changes in CMI, 4946 participants from the
CHARLS (mean age: 58.6 years, male: 46.4 %) and 2371 from the ELSA
(mean age: 63.1 years, male: 42.3 %) were included according to cor-
responding criteria. Baseline characteristics of these participants are
shown in Table 2. In addition, we also presented the baseline charac-
teristics of these participants using data not being imputed (Supple-
mental Tables 1 and 2). The results were consistent with those in
Tables 1 and 2.

3.3. Association of total cardiometabolic index with incident
cardiovascular disease
In the changes in CMI analyses, the median follow-up periods were
5.0 and 10.1 years in the CHARLS and ELSA, respectively. During the
follow-up, 897 incident CVD events (603 heart disease and 380 strokes)
in the CHARLS and 564 incident CVD events (491 heart disease and 111
strokes) in the ELSA were recorded. Table 4 displays the association
between total CMI with the risk of incident CVD. Participants with the
upper tertile of total CMI had significantly increased risks of incident
CVD compared to those with the lower tertile (CHARLS, HR 1.53, 95 %
CI 1.29–1.81; ELSA, HR 1.41, 95 % CI 1.15–1.73). For participants with
the middle tertile of total CMI, significantly increased CVD risks were
only observed in the CHARLS (HR 1.36, 95 % CI 1.14–1.61), but not in
the ELSA. Moreover, the trend tests indicated that there were increasing
trends of incident CVD risks with the increasing total CMI in the CHARLS
and ELSA (all P for trend <0.001). In the multivariable-adjusted RCS
regression models, there was a linear association was observed between
the total CMI and the risk of incident CVD in the ELSA (P for overall =
0.002; P for nonlinear = 0.534), while a positive nonlinear relationship
was observed in the CHARLS (P for overall <0.001; P for nonlinear =
0.017) (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion
In this study with two prospective cohorts, we examined the asso-
ciations of baseline and changes in CMI with the risk of incident CVD
(Graphical Abstract). Higher levels of the baseline CMI were associated
with an increased risk of incident CVD compared to lower levels.
Moreover, participants with higher levels of total CMI had elevated risks
of incident CVD. The dose-response curve between the baseline and total
CMI and CVD risk showed a nonlinear relationship in the CHARLS, while
a linear relationship in the ELSA. Therefore, monitoring CMI offers a
simple yet potent approach to CVD prevention strategies in clinical
practice.
CMI, a newly developed clinical marker, was first reported by
Wakabayashi et al. in 2015 and has been shown to have a significant
advantage in identifying diabetes [14] In a cohort study of 7347 par-
ticipants from China, participants with high levels of CMI had an
increased risk of incident diabetes than those with low levels (HR 1.78,
95 %CI 1.55–2.05) [37] Moreover, recent studies have confirmed CMI’s
linkage to increased risks of new-onset diabetes and expanded its rele-
vance to incident CVD [22,38] During a median follow-up of 6.8 years,
Cai et al. [22] in a retrospective cohort study enrolling 2067 participants
found a positive association between CMI and the risk of incident CVD in
patients with hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea (per standard
deviation increment, HR 1.31, 95 % CI 1.20–1.43). Consistently, our

In addition to the baseline CMI, our study additionally investigated
the associations of changes in CMI with incident CVD, which were not
examined previously. Both WHtR and TG/HDL-C ratio, which are uti-
lized to calculate the CMI, are considered time-varying exposures.
However, the above evidence [22,38] is susceptible to potential
regression dilution bias due to a single measurement of the CMI [42] It
has been suggested that the above limitations and methodological in-
sufficiencies can be addressed by assessing the impact of changes in CMI
on incident CVD risks. Total CMI, calculated by the CMI at baseline plus
the CMI at the second survey, was used to assess the changes in CMI. In
this study, we found that total CMI were associated with an increased
risk of incident CVD. The highest risk of incident CVD was observed in
participants

the

with

and HR of 1.41 (95 % CI, 1.15–1.73) in the ELSA. In addition, the risks
were not attenuated by several sensitivity analyses, which further
corroborated our previous findings.
Our study has important clinical and public health implications.
First, this study contributes to the existing knowledge of CVD risk pre-
vention and control by providing evidence supporting the utilization of
the baseline and total CMI as a clinically valuable marker for identifying
individuals at high risk of developing CVD. The CMI, calculated by TG,
HDL-C, WC, and height, can be conveniently obtained from a single
sample at the same time, even in primary healthcare settings, enhancing
the generalizability and practicality of CMI in both clinical and epide-
miological studies. For the general population, it is important to main-
tain appropriate levels of TG, HDL-C, and WC within the desirable range
for preventing chronic diseases. In addition, for those with hyperlipid-
emia and obesity, it is vital to encourage adhere to lipid-lowering and
weight-lowering drugs for actively controlling CMI levels. Therefore,
integrating the assessment of CMI into routine cardiovascular practice is
necessary. Furthermore, through dynamic monitoring of CMI, we can
not only assess the CVD risk of participants at any time but also adjust
the clinical interventions according to the changes in CMI at any time,
enabling precise medical treatment and reducing the CVD risk and the
burden of CVD.

There are some limitations in this study. First, the determination of
CVD relied on self-reported physician-diagnosed information, which
might lead to a misclassification bias. However, a previous study [43].
found that 77.5 % of self-reported coronary heart disease were consis-
tent with the medical records in the ELSA, which suggested that the
potential misclassification bias was minor. In addition, we performed
sensitivity analyses using the outcomes of heart disease and stroke
separately. These results were consistent with the main analyses. Sec-
ond, selection bias might occur for changes in CMI analyses as we further
excluded 5021 participants from the baseline CMI analyses. We
compared the baseline characteristics of included and excluded partic-
ipants (Supplemental Tables 18 and 19), indicating the existence of
healthy participant bias. Third, there was no information on statin
therapy in both databases, which had an impact on the lipid levels. Thus,
it is not clear whether the association between CMI and incident CVD
still existed among adults receiving statin therapy, and further re-
searches are needed to validate the association. Finally, despite adjust-
ing for multiple confounders, there might remain residual confounding
factors when analyzing two cohort with inherently different genetics,
cultures and locations, such as genetic susceptibility, diet, and pollution
levels. These may explain some of the differences in the relationship
between CMI and incident CVD risks between these two cohorts. Future
studies are needed to confirm our findings and explore the underlying
mechanism of the differences between two population groups.
5. Conclusions
In the current study, we found that both baseline and total CMI were
associated with an increased risk of CVD. Therefore, dynamic moni-
toring of CMI levels is vital for CVD prevention in clinical practice.
