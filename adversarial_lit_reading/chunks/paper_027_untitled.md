Abstract
Background and Objectives
Individuals with prevalent diabetes were known to have a higher risk of dementia and lower
cognitive function. However, trends of cognitive function before diabetes and in the short term
after new-onset diabetes remain unclear.
Methods
This study included participants without baseline diabetes from the China Health and Re-
tirement Longitudinal Study. Cognitive tests were conducted at baseline (wave 1) and at least
one time from wave 2 (2013) to wave 4 (2018). Cognitive function was assessed using a global
cognition score which was the summary measure of 4 cognitive tests. A linear mixed model was
constructed to ﬁt the trends in cognitive function before and after diabetes onset and the trends
among nondiabetes. The threshold of statistical signiﬁcance was p < 0.05.
Results
During the 7-year follow-up, 1,207 (9.7% of 12,422, 59.1 ± 8.6 years, 39.9% male participants)
participants developed new-onset diabetes. The cognitive function of both the without diabetes
group and the diabetes group declined annually during the follow-up. The annual decline rate of
the diabetes group before diabetes onset was similar to that of the without diabetes group
during the whole follow-up period. After diabetes onset, participants experienced statistically
signiﬁcant faster cognitive declines in global cognition (−0.023 SD/year; 95% CI −0.043 to
−0.004; p = 0.019) and visuospatial abilities test (−0.036 SD/year; −0.061 to −0.011; p =
0.004), but not in tests of episodic memory (−0.018 SD/year; −0.041 to 0.004; p = 0.116),
attention and calculation (−0.017 SD/year; −0.037 to 0.003; p = 0.090), or orientation (0.001
SD/year; −0.018 to 0.020; p = 0.894), compared with the cognitive slope before diabetes. In
subgroup analysis, compared with those who developed diabetes between 45–54 years, those
developing diabetes older (55–64 years, p for interaction = 0.701; 65–74 years, p for interaction =
0.996) did not demonstrate diﬀerent rates of global cognitive decline after diabetes.
Discussion
Individuals experienced faster rate of cognitive decline in a few years after diabetes onset, but
not during the prediabetes period. Age did not modify the eﬀect of diabetes on postdiabetes
cognitive decline. Eﬀorts in eliminating the adverse impacts on cognition should be started on
diagnosis of diabetes.

Introduction
Dementia currently aﬀects approximately 50 million people
worldwide. This number is predicted to increase to 152 million
by 2050.1 Owing to the lack of eﬀective treatments, modiﬁable
risk factors have drawn special attention for dementia pre-
vention. Diabetes is a key modiﬁable risk factor for dementia.2
Hyperglycemia contributes to dementia through both Alzheimer
disease–related and vascular disease–related pathology.3,4
Diabetes increased the risk of follow-up dementia.5 Meanwhile,
those with known diabetes had lower cognitive function cross-
sectionally and faster cognitive decline longitudinally.6,7 Most
studies only interviewed diabetes status at baseline, reﬂecting
the cognitive trends among participants with prevalent diabetes.
However, hyperglycemia might develop several years before the
cognitive assessment. Cognitive function before or in the short
period after diabetes onset is unknown. Considering that pre-
diabetes could also aﬀect cognitive health, cognitive de-
terioration might begin even before diabetes.8 Dementia is a
syndrome characterized by declines in cognitive function. Un-
derstanding the cognitive trajectories before and in the short
period after new-onset diabetes could help unravel the patho-
physiology of diabetes on the brain and the prevention and
management of dementia in diabetic individuals.
Few studies have indeed reported cognitive trends after9-13 or
before and after new-onset diabetes.14,15 However, several
knowledge gaps remain unaddressed. First, the study pop-
ulation was relatively older in the previous 2 studies which
calculated the cognitive trends before and after diabetes.14,15
A recent article published in the JAMA reported younger age
at onset of diabetes was even associated with higher risk of
subsequent dementia.16 Therefore, whether individuals with
lower mean age exhibited diﬀerent magnitude of cognitive
decline is not clear. Second, to our knowledge, cognitive
trajectories before and after diabetes onset have not been
investigated in the Chinese population, who have diﬀerent
educational and economic levels. This might contribute to
diﬀerent cognitive reserve and thus diﬀerent patterns of
cognitive change.8 Third, previous studies achieved mixed
results in test-speciﬁc cognitive trends. For example, some
reported accelerated declines in memory function after di-
abetes onset,14,15 while some reported that incident diabetes
was not associated with longitudinal memory decline.10,11
In this study, we aimed to explore the temporal pattern of
declines in cognitive function before and after diabetes onset
among middle-aged and older Chinese population (45 years
or older).
Methods
Study Participants
This study used data from wave 1 to wave 4 of the China
Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a
nationally representative and community-based cohort study
in China.17 At baseline (deﬁned as wave 1), 17,708 partici-
pants were recruited from 150 national units distributed in 28
provinces of China. The wave 1 survey was performed be-
tween June 2011 and March 2012. Three follow-up visits
(wave 2, 2013; wave 3, 2015; and wave 4, 2018) were con-
ducted after wave 1.
The ﬂowchart for sample selection is shown in Figure 1. Of
the 16,040 participants who ﬁnished cognitive assessments at
baseline, 1,196 were excluded for the following reasons: under
45 years at baseline and having a history of brain injuries or
psychiatric disorders, stroke, or memory-related diseases
which included Alzheimer disease, brain atrophy, and Par-
kinson disease. Subsequently, 2,422 of the remaining 14,844
individuals were further excluded due to diabetes history at
baseline (n = 1,170), lack of baseline covariates (n = 43), or
not receiving at least one cognitive assessment during the
follow-up from wave 2 to wave 4 (n = 1,377).
Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations,
and Patient Consents
CHARLS was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of
Peking University (IRB00001052–11014 and IRB00001052-
11015). Each participant had signed 2 copies of informed
consent (eAppendix 1, links.lww.com/WNL/D458).
Assessment of Cognitive Function
Cognitive function was evaluated in each wave and included
the following 4 tests: episodic memory, visuospatial abilities,
orientation, and attention and calculation. The episodic
memory was tested by word recall, and its total score was
composed of the correct number of immediate and delayed (5
minutes) word recalls. The score ranged from 0 to 10 (0.5
points for each correct word). Visuospatial abilities were
evaluated by replicating 2 overlapping pentagons in a drawing.
Participants who completed the task successfully received 1
point. The orientation abilities were assessed by asking par-
ticipants to identify the date (year, month, day), day of the
week, and season (score ranging, 0–5). The attention and

Assessment of Diabetes Onset
Diabetes was deﬁned as self-reported physician’s diagnosis,
antidiabetic medication use, or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)
≥6.5%. Information on self-reported diagnosis and medica-
tion use was available in all 4 waves. The HbA1c level was
measured in wave 1 and wave 3. For some participants, the
time of diabetes onset was recorded through interviews
(“When was diabetes ﬁrst diagnosed or known?”). For other
participants, the time of diabetes onset was recorded as the
midpoint between the last time without diabetes and the ﬁrst
time with diabetes. The study did not distinguish between
type 1 and type 2 diabetes.
Covariates
Information on sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors,
and health-related variables was collected. Our analysis ad-
justed baseline covariates associated with diabetes or cognitive
function, including age, sex, education, marital status, resi-
dential area, smoking, drinking, the number of instrumental
activities of daily living (IADLs), hypertension, high total
cholesterol, lung diseases, heart problems, cancer, and de-
pressive symptoms. Details of covariates are available in
eAppendix 2 (links.lww.com/WNL/D458).17
Statistical Analysis
Baseline characteristics were compared between participants
who developed diabetes during the follow-up and those free of
diabetes until the end of the follow-up. We constructed a linear
mixed-eﬀect model, including both ﬁxed eﬀects and random
eﬀects, to explore the longitudinal data with repeated
measurement.20,21 Time and participant are the level 1 and
level 2 variables, respectively. We ﬁtted ﬁxed eﬀects for in-
tercept, diabetes (with or without during the follow-up), years
since baseline, “‘diabetes*years since baseline” interaction,
“diabetes*diabetes status*years after diabetes” interaction, and
all covariates. Intercept and slope (years since baseline and
years after diabetes) are ﬁtted for both ﬁxed eﬀects and random
eﬀects. The variable “diabetes” was set to 0 for the control
group and 1 for the diabetes group. The ﬁxed eﬀect parameter
estimate for “diabetes” could reﬂect the baseline diﬀerence
between the diabetes group and the control group. The “years
after diabetes” was set to 0 for the control group and for the
diabetes group during the prediabetes period, and it indicated
whether the average cognitive slope changed after diabetes
onset compared with the average slope before diabetes. After

Sensitivity Analysis
For tests of global cognition, episodic memory, and attention
and calculation, the cognitive decline did not follow a linear
trend (eFigure 3, links.lww.com/WNL/D458, data not
shown). The nonlinearity could be generated by practice ef-
fect at prior waves, which made the prediabetes rate of change
of the diabetes group look more positive than it actually was.
eFigure 4 shows 4 possible cognitive patterns. In eFigure 4C,
if simply compared the cognitive slope in later times with the
slope in early times among the diabetes group, we would
achieve false-positive results that diabetes was correlated with
steeper cognitive decline. Sensitivity analysis is needed to
determine whether the case belonged to eFigure 4C or D. In
all cognitive tests, scores of the diabetes group and the control
group were similar at baseline (reported by the baseline
comparison table and the linear mixed model). Therefore, as a
sensitivity analysis for prediabetes diﬀerence, we compared
the prediabetes cognitive slope with the slope of the control
group from 0 to 2 years, on the ground of the average pre-
diabetes follow-up time. Moreover, we veriﬁed whether the
score change between the ﬁrst and the last time point (wave 1
and wave 4) diﬀered between the diabetes group and the
control group as a sensitivity analysis for postdiabetes cog-
nitive change.
The diabetes group had longer follow-ups because they were
“about to be diagnosed with diabetes.” All diabetes partici-
pants were alive before the time of diabetes onset, but par-
ticipants in the control group might lose to follow-up or have
already died at the same time. Those lost to follow-up had
poorer cognitive scores (eTable 1, links.lww.com/WNL/
D458), probably making us underestimate the cognitive
scores of the control group before diabetes. Therefore, we
conducted a match analysis in which participants who de-
veloped diabetes (case) during follow-up were matched with
3 randomly selected participants from the control group
without diabetes (control) who were still alive in the earliest
wave after the diabetes onset. Matching was based on sex and
same age (±1 year) of cases and their matched controls at the
diabetes time. To evaluate the stability of our primary results,
the main analysis was repeated separately using the following
procedures: (1) Adding “diabetes*diabetes status” as a ﬁxed
eﬀect in the linear mixed model. The “diabetes status” is a
time-varying dichotomous variable that changed from “0” to
“1” at the time of diabetes onset and reﬂected whether the
cognitive function declined acutely at diabetes onset.23,24 A
detailed description of this variable is presented in eFigure 5
and eAppendix 2.25 (2) Further adjusting for body mass index
(BMI). Since few participants had BMI data in wave 1,
measures of BMI in wave 2 were used as the estimates in wave
1 in this analysis. (3) Excluding participants who had a stroke
during the follow-up. (4) Restricting participants to those
who had complete cognitive data in all 4 waves.
All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4
(SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Two-sided p values below 0.05
were considered statistically signiﬁcant.

When comparing the slope between 2 time points (0-year/
wave 1 and 2-year/wave 2) before diabetes, the average pre-
diabetes cognitive slope of the diabetes group was similar to
that of the control group (eTable 9, links.lww.com/WNL/
D458). We then compared the slope between wave 1 and
wave 4. The mean global cognitive slope of the diabetes group
was less than that of the control group (β, −0.049 SD; 95% CI
−0.085 to −0.013; p = 0.008; eTable 10). This is in accordance
with our primary result that global cognition deteriorated after
new-onset diabetes. Similar results were found in tests of
episodic memory (a tendency to deteriorate after diabetes),
visuospatial abilities (deteriorated after diabetes), and orien-
tation (did not change after diabetes). However, for the at-
tention and calculation test, the slope between wave 1 and
wave 4 was similar between the 2 groups (β, 0.003 SD; 95% CI
−0.033 to 0.040; p = 0.848; eTable 10), suggesting that its
decline tendency was ‘false-positive.’
In the match analysis, the diabetes group did not exhibit
prediabetes cognitive disadvantage compared with the
match group without diabetes. However, the postdiabetes
cognitive decline of global cognition score turned out to be
statistically insigniﬁcant (β, −0.021 SD/year; 95% CI −0.042
to 0.001; p = 0.059; eTable 11, links.lww.com/WNL/D458).
We did not observe “acute cognitive decline/change” at the
time of diabetes onset (global cognition; β, 0.007 SD/year;
95% CI −0.055 to 0.069; p = 0.828, eTable 12). After adding
BMI as a covariate, excluding participants who had a stroke
during the follow-up, or restricting participants to those
receiving cognitive measurements in all 4 waves, we achieved
constant results that scores of global cognition and visuo-
spatial abilities declined gradually before diabetes and
showed a faster deterioration rate in the years after diabetes
onset (eTables 13–15).
Discussion
In this population-based study of Chinese adults aged 45 years
or older, participants experienced faster rates of decline in
global cognition and visuospatial ability scores several years
after diabetes onset, compared with the rate before diabetes.
However, the postdiabetes cognitive decline was not signiﬁ-
cantly steeper in tests of episodic memory, attention and
calculation, or orientation. The rate of decline before diabetes
onset among those with diabetes was similar to the rate of
individuals free of diabetes.
The cognitive trajectory before and after new-onset diabetes
was relatively unexplored, especially in Asia. Several longitu-
dinal studies have assessed their participants’ cognitive func-
tion more than once and calculated the cognitive trends of
those who developed new-onset diabetes during the follow-up
period.9-13 However, they rarely analyze whether the trends in
cognition after diabetes onset diﬀered from trends before
diabetes. To our knowledge, only 2 studies compared the
cognitive trajectory before and after diabetes onset. A Chicago
Health and Aging Project (CHAP) study reported faster
postdiabetes declines in Mini-Mental State Examination
(MMSE) scores, executive function, and episodic memory.14
A recent study using data from the English Longitudinal Study
of Ageing (ELSA) suggested accelerated declines in tests of
global cognition, orientation, memory, and executive function
after diabetes onset. A key ﬁnding of our study lies in the
diﬀerent patterns of cognitive decline. Diﬀerent from the
ELSA study from the United Kingdom, the orientation ability
scores in our study did not deteriorate after diabetes.15 We
were unable to explore the diﬀerence. Future neuroimaging
studies could learn the reasons for diﬀerent cognitive tests. In
addition, this study reported the slope of visuospatial abilities
steeper after diabetes, providing a fuller picture of the cog-
nitive patterns. Our results of the episodic memory and at-
tention and calculation did not meet our threshold of
signiﬁcance, which might be owing to diﬀerent disease dura-
tion and the length of follow-up. In accordance with the
previous 2 studies, accelerated cognitive decline occurs after
but not before diabetes onset. Close monitoring of cognitive
dysfunction in patients with diabetes is vitally important in
short periods after a diabetes diagnosis.

Another important ﬁnding was our subgroup analyses by age.
A Whitehall II study linked every 5-year younger age at di-
abetes onset to a higher risk of dementia at age 70 years
(hazard ratio, 1.24; 95% CI 1.06–1.46).16 This intrigued us to
investigate whether younger diabetes patients had increased
cognitive decline compared with older patients. However, we
did not ﬁnd age groups modifying the eﬀect of diabetes onset
on the changes in cognitive slope after diabetes. The previous
Whitehall II study collected data on diabetes onset and de-
mentia diagnosis from 1985 to 2019. The follow-up period of
CHARLS is relatively shorter. Our study could be updated
after CHALRS releases its new survey in the future and ex-
plore whether new ﬁndings could be achieved after the
timeline is prolonged.
Although the exact pathophysiologic pathway through which
diabetes contributes to cognitive decline beyond aging re-
mains unclear, several mechanisms have been proposed.26
Increasing evidence suggests that insulin resistance might be a
key factor. Brain insulin resistance may lead to impairments in
synaptic, metabolic, and immune response functions, which
further damage cognitive function.27 Moreover, diabetes in-
duces Alzheimer disease–related pathology, such as β-amyloid
neurotic plaques (NPs) and intracellular neuroﬁbrillary tan-
gles (NFTs).28 Another vital mechanism linking hyperglyce-
mia to cognitive impairment is various vascular damages such
as stroke, large vessel arteriosclerosis, small vessel arterio-
sclerosis, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Studies also
reported that increased white matter hyperintensities and la-
cunar infarcts were observed in diabetes patients, which were
associated with cognitive impairment.26,27,29,30 In addition,
oxidative stress, neuroinﬂammation, and some risk factors
accompanying diabetes such as hypertension, obesity, and
depression all lead to cognition impairment.26,31-34 In sum-
mary, multiple mechanisms jointly contribute to brain injury,
eventually resulting in postdiabetes cognitive decline. Pre-
vious stroke studies reported that stroke patients experienced
an acute decline at stroke onset but not accelerated future
decline in global cognition.23,24 By contrast, diabetes patients
did not exhibit “acute cognitive decline,” illustrating that the
adverse impact of diabetes-related pathology on the brain
tends to be a gradual process, instead of a sudden brain injury
such as stroke.
This study has several advantages. It is one of the largest
studies exploring the cognitive trends during the prediabetes
period and in the years after new-onset diabetes. The large
sample size with repeated measurements oﬀered us the op-
portunity to generate precise and reliable trajectories of
cognitive function. Furthermore, our results extend the
ﬁndings of previous studies by subgroup analyses of younger
participants.
A few limitations of this study should be mentioned. First, the
follow-up period of CHARLS is relatively short. Diabetes is a
life-long condition. Our results could only reﬂect the cognitive
trends in the short term after diabetes. The cognitive
assessments are limited, and diabetes diagnoses for part of the
participants are imprecise. Many of our participants were from
rural areas. The generalizability to other populations or the
long term after diabetes might be a concern. Second, for part
of cognitive tests, relationships between time and scores were
nonlinear. Accordingly, the linear mixed model could only
illustrate that participants experienced a slight cognitive de-
terioration after developing diabetes but could not visualize
the actual cognitive trends. Because of limited follow-up
times, we were unable to delete or adjust for baseline scores to
mitigate the practice eﬀects which led to a boost in perfor-
mance after the ﬁrst test.35 Third, from wave 1 to wave 3, the
CHARLS lack tests such as MMSE, which precluded us from
deﬁning dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) by
using cut-oﬀpoints of cognitive scores.36,37 Among our par-
ticipants, the cognitive trajectories were similar across diﬀerent
age subgroups. However, we were unable to study whether
younger participants had higher risk of dementia, which is a
topical research area. Fourth, CHARLS only performed blood
tests in wave 1 and wave 3. A certain proportion of diabetes
patients were unaware of their glucose level, leading to a delay
in diagnosis of diabetes. Future studies could conduct fasting
blood tests more frequently. Fifth, although a variety of con-
founding factors were adjusted, there may still be some related
but uncontrolled variables such as apolipoprotein E (ApoE)
genotype and air pollution. The frequency of the ApoE e4 allele
is approximately 7% in the Chinese population.38 Conse-
quently, the genotype may have fewer eﬀects on our results.
In conclusion, our ﬁndings demonstrated that new-onset di-
abetes was associated with a faster rate of future cognitive decline,
but not during the prediabetes period. Eﬀorts in eliminating the
adverse impacts on cognition should be started on diagnosis of
diabetes. Future studies are needed to learn the mechanisms of
cognitive decline in a few years after new-onset diabetes.
